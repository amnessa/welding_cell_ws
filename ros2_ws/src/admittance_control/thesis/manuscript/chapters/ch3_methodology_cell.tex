Tack welding fixes the parts of an assembly in place before the final weld, and a robot can
place tacks only if it knows where the parts are. In this chapter, we describe a robotic cell
that turns a hand-placed assembly of known parts into marked tack positions. The cell uses one
RGB-D camera on the robot's wrist and localises the parts by vision alone.
Section~\ref{sec:mc_overview} gives the system as a whole and Section~\ref{sec:mc_problem}
states the problem and its notation. The sections after them follow the pipeline: calibration
(\S\ref{sec:mc_calibration}), the operator interface (\S\ref{sec:mc_hri}), perception and
registration (\S\ref{sec:mc_perception}), multi-view refinement (\S\ref{sec:mc_multiview}),
seams (\S\ref{sec:mc_seams}), tacks (\S\ref{sec:mc_tacks}) and motion (\S\ref{sec:mc_motion}).
Implementation details close the chapter (\S\ref{sec:mc_impl}); the measured performance of
every component is reported in Chapter~\ref{chp:results}.

Two scope decisions shape the chapter. First, a pen replaces the welding torch. The pen must
reach the same points along the same axes as a torch, with the same clearance from the parts,
so the geometry, the reach and the accuracy requirements carry over unchanged; the heat does
not. Welding itself, and the distortion it causes, are outside the scope of this thesis; heat enters
only as a possible parameter of the order in which tacks are laid (\S\ref{sec:mc_tacks}), not as a
model of its effect on the material.
Second, several components are pretrained models or libraries that we did not write: SAM2,
FoundationPose, MediaPipe, OpenCV's point-pair features, OMPL and MoveIt's time
parameterisation. Rather than describing how these tools were built, we explain what each one
computes, what we give it, what it returns and how its output is used, at the point of use.
Their architectures are summarised only as far as needed to understand their inputs, outputs
and failure modes.

\section{Overview of the Proposed System}
\label{sec:mc_overview}
The cell works on assemblies of parts whose CAD models are known: plates, tubes, mitred and
saddle-cut tubes, a rounded-rectangle section and a curved band, placed by hand on a table in
weld position, one after the other. For every part, the system finds its location and
orientation, computes where the parts meet, places tacks on the weldable seams and marks each
tack with a pen held by a UR5e.

The design rests on one principle, the same one that underlies WeldSet
(Chapter~\ref{chp:method_weldset}): \emph{seams and tacks are computed from exact geometry,
never detected in the point cloud}. The camera's only job is to measure the poses of known
parts. Everything downstream (seams, tacks, reachability, motion) is geometry on exact
primitives, so its only error is the registration's. The pipeline has four stages
(Figure~\ref{fig:mc_pipeline}):
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Perceive} (per part). The operator aims the camera by pointing, freezes a frame
  and clicks the part with gestures. The part is segmented, identified in the CAD library and
  registered in 6D, then tracked while it is placed and saved when it comes to rest.
\item \textbf{Refine} (once, all parts saved). The robot moves the camera to four close views
  around the joints, and the poses of all parts are refined together, with the camera's
  extrinsic translation estimated on the way.
\item \textbf{Compute.} Each registered part is represented as an exact geometric primitive.
  Seams follow from where the primitives meet, and tacks from a placement rule on the seams.
\item \textbf{Act.} Reachability on one arm configuration, collision-free transits and a
  force-gated descent bring the pen to every tack.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Vision only.} Welding robots often localise a part by touch sensing: the wire or
the gas nozzle is driven onto the part until an electrical contact closes, and the contact
points correct the programmed path. Touch sensing is accurate, but it is slow (one approach per
measured direction), it needs conductive parts and a free path to every probed surface, and it
ties localisation to the tool. In this thesis, we exclude it by design: a scenario in which the
parts cannot be touched must remain valid. Touches are used in two roles only: to calibrate the
cell against the robot (\S\ref{sec:mc_calibration}), and to measure, after a tack is marked,
how far the pen met the real surface from where the registration put it
(Equation~\ref{eq:touch}). Neither role feeds a correction back into a tack. Therefore, every
correction applied to a tack in this thesis comes from the camera.

\paragraph{Two computers.} The work is split by what each stage needs. Registration is rare and

Only one frame per registration crosses the network (\S\ref{sec:mc_impl}).

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:mc_problem}
\textbf{Given} a library of CAD models $\{\mathcal{M}_m\}$, a UR5e manipulator with joint
vector $\vect{q}\in\mathbb{R}^6$ and forward kinematics $\Tf{B}{E}(\vect{q})$, an RGB-D camera
rigidly mounted on the wrist, and an assembly of parts $k=1,\dots,K$ placed by hand,
\textbf{find} for every part its model $m_k$ and pose $\Tf{B}{O_k}$; from the poses, the set of
weldable seams $\mathcal{S}=\{\mathcal{S}_j\}$; on the seams, a set of tacks, each a point
$\vect{p}_i$ with a pen axis $\vect{a}_i$; and for every tack a collision-free joint trajectory
that brings the pen tip to $\vect{p}_i$ along $\vect{a}_i$. Table~\ref{tab:notation} collects
the notation used in this chapter and the next.

\label{tab:notation}

Symbol & Meaning \\
\midrule
$B$, $E$, $C$, $P$, $O_k$ & frames: robot base (\texttt{base\_link}), flange (\texttt{tool0}), camera colour optical frame, pen tip (the TCP), CAD frame of part $k$ \\

The quantity that matters is the mark error: the distance between where the pen marks and the
true weld root. It accumulates along the chain of frames that turns a camera measurement into a
robot target,
\begin{equation}
\Tf{B}{O}(t) = \Tf{B}{E}\big(\vect{q}(t)\big)\;\Tf{E}{C}\;\Tf{C}{O}(t),
\qquad
\Tf{B}{P}(t) = \Tf{B}{E}\big(\vect{q}(t)\big)\;\Tf{E}{P},
\label{eq:frame_chain}
\end{equation}
where $\Tf{B}{E}$ comes from the robot's kinematics (\S\ref{sec:cal_kin}), $\Tf{E}{C}$ from the
hand-eye calibration and its refinements (\S\ref{sec:mc_handeye}--\S\ref{sec:cal_trans}),
$\Tf{C}{O}$ from registration (\S\ref{sec:mc_perception}) and $\Tf{E}{P}$ from the tool-centre
calibration (\S\ref{sec:cal_tcp}). The pen closes the chain at the far end: it measures where
the surface really is, through the same kinematics, but without the camera. The links can thus
be checked one by one, in the order
\emph{camera depth $\to$ extrinsic $\to$ kinematics $\to$ registration $\to$ seam $\to$ planner
$\to$ pen tip}, and the rule we follow is to measure a link against something that does not
depend on it before changing it.

Because the camera is on the wrist, $\Tf{B}{C}$ changes with every motion, and three design
rules follow from Equation~\ref{eq:frame_chain}. Everything that must stay put (the saved parts,
the static environment) is stored in $B$, never in $C$. Every lookup of $\Tf{B}{E}$ uses the
time stamp of the image it serves, not the current time. And a mask or a click is valid only for
the frame it was made on.

\paragraph{Assumptions.} The parts are rigid and match their CAD models within the
manufacturing tolerance. Each part rests on the table or on another part; this is used once,
to settle the spin of a cut tube (\S\ref{sec:per_symmetry}), while the refinement deliberately
does not impose contact (\S\ref{sec:mc_multiview}). The table is a plane. The CAD library holds
every part. The operator places the parts one at a time, and nothing moves while the robot
refines or marks.

\section{Coordinate Frames and Calibration}
\label{sec:mc_calibration}
Every tack inherits the errors of every link of Equation~\ref{eq:frame_chain}, so the goal of
calibration is to make each link accurate in the frame the robot acts in. The principle is to
measure each link against a reference that does not depend on it. The robot is that reference:
its factory-calibrated kinematics and its pen tip, touched onto surfaces, measure better than
any camera-based estimate of the same quantity. The order matters too, because a step
calibrated on top of an uncorrected earlier one learns that earlier error. We observed this
once: before the depth was calibrated, the extrinsic self-calibration recorded the depth bias as
an offset of the camera. The chain is therefore run in the order of
Table~\ref{tab:calib_chain}, and each step is checked before the next one starts.

\subsection{Kinematics}
\label{sec:cal_kin}
The UR5e's controller computes its tool pose with a kinematic model calibrated at the factory
for the individual robot, while the URDF used by ROS and by our planners describes the nominal
robot. In our application the two differ by 2.4--4.2~mm and about $0.5^\circ$ at the tool,
depending on the pose, which alone put the first marks about 4~mm off (Chapter~\ref{chp:results}).
The difference was measured by a probe that compares the controller's own TCP pose with both
models at every pose. Therefore, the factory calibration, extracted from the controller, is
loaded into the URDF, the driver and every tool of the cell, and every report records which
model it used. This step has no free parameter: it enforces that every consumer of a frame uses
one kinematic model.
\SRC{config/ur5e\_calibration.yaml; urdf/ur5e.urdf.xacro; kinematics.use\_kinematics;
scripts/tcp\_offset\_probe.py; thesis\_notes.md 2026-09-29.}

\subsection{Pen Tool Centre Point}
\label{sec:cal_tcp}
The pen tip is the tool centre point, $\vect{d}=\vect{t}_{EP}$ in $E$. It is found by the
classic four-point method: the tip is held on one fixed point $\vect{p}$ from $n\ge4$ clearly
different wrist orientations $(\mathbf{R}_i,\vect{t}_i)$, so that
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{R}_i\,\vect{d}+\vect{t}_i=\vect{p}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_i & -\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}\vect{d}\\ \vect{p}\end{bmatrix}=-\vect{t}_i,\qquad i=1,\dots,n,
\label{eq:tcp_4pt}
\end{equation}
is solved by least squares; the residual is the repeatability. We use the pendant's own
implementation, so that the controller and every ROS tool share one TCP.

The result is checked against the table plane $(\vect{n},d)$ of \S\ref{sec:cal_table}. If the
real tip is off by $\vect{e}$ (in $E$), a touch recorded at $\vect{p}_i$ with orientation
$\mathbf{R}_i$ has its real tip at $\vect{p}_i+\mathbf{R}_i\vect{e}$, which lies on the plane:
\begin{equation}
(\mathbf{R}_i^{\top}\vect{n})^{\top}\vect{e}+\delta=-h_i,
\qquad h_i=\vect{n}^{\top}\vect{p}_i+d,
\label{eq:tcp_check}
\end{equation}
with $\delta$ a free offset, since the plane was measured with the same TCP. Vertical touches
see only the component of $\vect{e}$ along $\vect{n}$; the lateral components become observable
only through the tilt, with a sensitivity of $\sin\vartheta$. The check therefore touches one
spot vertically at four rolls (a correct TCP leaves one dot) and tilted by $30^\circ$ towards
four azimuths with the tool yaw held, and it reports the conditioning of the lateral columns.
The along-pen component is weakly conditioned even at $30^\circ$, so a doubt about the pen's
length calls for $45^\circ$ tilts.
\SRC{tool\_model.solve\_tip\_offset; table\_probe.solve\_tcp\_error, check\_orientations;
scripts/table\_touchoff.py mode:=tcp\_check; config/pen\_tool.json (TCP in use, 2026-09-28).}

\subsection{Table Plane}
\label{sec:cal_table}
The table is the height reference of the whole cell: the point cloud is cut 2~mm above it, the
depth and extrinsic checks measure against it, the Tare's distance is computed to it, and the
collision model stops every motion at it. It is measured by seven pen touches, a regular hexagon
with 10~cm edges and its centre, so that the fit has four spare points and a meaningful residual.
Each touch is made twice: fast (4~mm/s) to find the table, then, after backing off 3~mm, slowly
(1~mm/s) for the recorded point, which is the controller's TCP pose at the moment
$\norm{\vect{F}-\vect{F}_{\mathrm{bias}}}$ exceeds 1.5~N. The two speeds exist because the pen is
not sprung: on a rigid table the force past the threshold grows as speed $\times$ stopping
time $\times$ stiffness, and a first run at 10~mm/s read about 10~N once stopped. The plane is
fitted by least squares on height,
\begin{equation}
z=a\,x+b\,y+c,\qquad
\vect{n}=\frac{(-a,\,-b,\,1)^{\top}}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+1}},\qquad
d=\frac{-c}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+1}},
\label{eq:table_plane}
\end{equation}
and its tilt $\arccos n_z$ is kept, not removed: the table is not level, and a flat height cut
under a tilted table left part of the table in the cloud under the parts.
\SRC{table\_probe.touch\_pattern, fit\_plane; scripts/table\_touchoff.py; notebooks/table\_plane.json;
README \S14 (28 Sep: the flat ground cut).}

\subsection{Hand-Eye Calibration}
\label{sec:mc_handeye}
The camera's pose on the flange, $\mathbf{X}=\Tf{E}{C}$, is first estimated from views of a
calibration board. With $\mathbf{A}_i$ the relative motion of the robot between two samples and
$\mathbf{B}_i$ the relative motion of the board seen by the camera,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}_i\,\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{B}_i,
\label{eq:handeye}
\end{equation}
which is solved by Tsai and Lenz's method \cite{tsai1989handeye} where OpenCV provides it and by
Park and Martin's \cite{park1994handeye} (the rotation from a least-squares fit in the
logarithmic map, then the translation linearly) otherwise. The board is a ChArUco board of
$4\times4$ squares of 30~mm with 22~mm markers, its pose found by PnP on the interpolated
corners; the robot's pose comes from the controller at the pendant's active TCP. The solution is
therefore the camera in the TCP frame, and it must be composed into the flange frame,
$\Tf{E}{C}=\Tf{E}{P}\,\Tf{P}{C}$; the tools refuse to save a result without the TCP offset.

Every solve is judged by its own consistency: the board's pose in the base,
$\Tf{B}{\mathrm{board},i}=\Tf{B}{P,i}\,\mathbf{X}\,\Tf{C}{\mathrm{board},i}$, must be the same for
every sample. The spread of its position (standard deviation and range) and the RMS of its
rotation about the chordal mean are printed after every solve, and a result is graded good below
6~mm and $2^\circ$. However, this residual has a blind spot that the cell exposed three times.
A solve once failed silently inside a library call and was used for weeks because nothing printed
its residual; a solve left in the TCP frame fitted its samples perfectly, because a rigid change
of the gripper frame is invisible to Equation~\ref{eq:handeye}, and put the camera 129~mm behind
the pen tip; and a rotation bias of about $0.36^\circ$ sat inside the solve's own uncertainty, about
$0.3^\circ$ ($1.8^\circ$ per view over 33 views), and kept every recalibration wandering by a few
millimetres. A calibration residual only says that a solution fits its own samples. Each of the
three was found by comparing against an independent reference, the pen. Therefore, the hand-eye
result is used only as the starting point: its rotation is refined against the table
(\S\ref{sec:cal_rot}) and its translation online (\S\ref{sec:mv_selfcal}).
\SRC{helper/calibration/extract\_extrinsics.py, resolve\_handeye.py; notebooks/handeye\_samples.npz
(33 samples); README \S14 (24 and 28 Sep); pen\_marking\_plan.md M5.}

\subsection{Depth Calibration Against the Robot}
\label{sec:mc_depth}
A stereo camera computes depth from disparity, $z=fb/d_{\mathrm{px}}$, so a disparity offset of
$\delta$ pixels, with focal length $f$ and baseline $b$, gives a depth error that grows with the
square of the range:
\begin{equation}
\Delta z \approx \frac{z^{2}\,\delta}{f\,b}.
\label{eq:depth_error}
\end{equation}
The symptom that led to this model was that every pen mark met the surface early, by about
8.5~mm, on both sides of a T-joint. Early contact on both sides points to a vertical error,
not a sideways one. The close views of the multi-view refinement (\S\ref{sec:mc_multiview})
then showed a range dependence: the parts read about 6.9~mm low from the scan pose at 0.6~m and
about 2.8~mm low from the close views at 0.4~m. That ratio, 2.5, matches a quadratic law (2.6)
better than a linear one (1.8). A fit $\Delta z=a+kz^2$ at three ranges confirmed it and implies
$\delta\approx0.6$~px (Chapter~\ref{chp:results}). The lesson is that a depth check at one
distance says little: several views at different ranges turn a hidden bias into a measurable
inconsistency.

The vendor's correction, the Tare, rescales the depth to a ground-truth distance that the user
enters. Entered by hand, it overshot into a linear scale error of about 1\,\%, probably because
the distance was measured from the camera's front glass rather than from its depth origin
4.2~mm behind it (a hypothesis we did not confirm). The vendor's Tare is only as good as its
ground-truth distance. In this thesis, the distance is computed from the robot instead: with the
camera centre $\vect{c}$ and optical axis $\hat{\vect{z}}_C$ in $B$ from
Equation~\ref{eq:frame_chain}, and the touched plane $(\vect{n},d)$, the depth along the optical
axis is
\begin{equation}
z^{*}=-\frac{\vect{n}^{\top}\vect{c}+d}{\vect{n}^{\top}\hat{\vect{z}}_C},
\label{eq:tare_distance}
\end{equation}
taken with the camera square to the table (within $1^\circ$) and its axis on the touched patch.
The camera then agrees with the frame the robot acts in. The result is checked at three ranges
over the same spot, spanning the working volume (0.3--0.65~m).
\SRC{scripts/tare\_distance.py, extrinsic\_check.py; table\_probe.surface\_vs\_plane;
thesis\_notes.md 2026-10-02 (depth); README \S16.}

\subsection{Extrinsic Rotation Against the Table}
\label{sec:cal_rot}
Seen from one pose, a camera tilted on the flange and a table tilted in the world look the same.
They separate when the wrist turns: the camera's tilt turns with the wrist yaw $\psi_i$, the
table's does not. With $\vect{t}_i\in\mathbb{R}^2$ the small-angle tilt of the table seen in view
$i$,
\begin{equation}
\vect{t}_i=\vect{a}+\mathbf{R}(\psi_i)\,\vect{b},
\label{eq:tilt_split}
\end{equation}
with $\vect{a}$ the table's own tilt and $\vect{b}$ the camera's, solved by least squares once the
yaws span at least $90^\circ$ (we use four, about $90^\circ$ apart). The camera's rotation
correction $\vect{w}=(w_x,w_y,0)$, in $C$, then follows from the observed normals to first order,
$\vect{n}_{\mathrm{obs},i}-\vect{n}_0\approx\vect{a}'+\vect{n}_0\times(\mathbf{R}_i\vect{w})$, and is
applied as $\Tf{E}{C}\leftarrow\Tf{E}{C}\operatorname{diag}(\exp[\vect{w}]_\times,1)$. Only the two
tilt angles are observable from a plane seen from above; the rotation about the optical axis and
the translation are not, and they are left as they were. The corrected extrinsic is written to a
new file and promoted by hand after a re-check shows the camera-fixed tilt near zero.
\SRC{table\_probe.separate\_tilt, refine\_camera\_rotation; scripts/extrinsic\_check.py;
helper/calibration/refine\_extrinsic\_from\_table.py; thesis\_notes.md 2026-10-02.}

\subsection{Extrinsic Translation}
\label{sec:cal_trans}
The translation of $\Tf{E}{C}$ is the hardest link to measure: a plane does not see it, and a
single view cannot tell it from the pose of the part. It becomes observable when one part is seen
from several directions, because an error in it shifts each view's cloud in a different
direction. It is therefore estimated inside the multi-view refinement, on every run
(\S\ref{sec:mv_selfcal}). Each run removes its own estimate from its own data; the calibration
file itself is corrected only from several agreeing runs, and promoted by hand.

\paragraph{Verification without correction.} Four probes check the chain without changing it,
and they are part of the measurement procedure of Chapter~\ref{chp:setup}:
\begin{itemize}
\item the pen's height above the touched plane and its signed distance to every registered face;
\item the live cloud's offset from every registered CAD face, and its drift across the face;
\item the noise floor of a static part's pose over 30~s;
\item after marking, the real part's offset fitted to the recorded pen contacts.
\end{itemize}

\section{Operator Interaction}
\label{sec:mc_hri}
In our application, the operator places parts by hand next to the robot, so the hands are busy
and a keyboard is out of reach. The goal is an interface that needs nothing but the hand and the
camera that is already on the wrist. Gestures seen by that camera drive the pipeline, and
pointing aims the camera before a frame is captured, so the operator never touches a keyboard
during a cycle.

\subsection{Gesture Interface}
\label{sec:mc_gestures}
The interface must recognise a small vocabulary of static hand commands and locate the index
fingertip, in real time, from the same colour stream that images the parts. A pendant or a
keyboard needs a free hand; speech is unreliable next to machines; a second camera for the hand
would need its own calibration to the first. We use MediaPipe's Gesture Recognizer
\cite{mediapipe_gesture_recognizer}, built on MediaPipe Hands \cite{zhang2020mediapipehands} in
the MediaPipe framework \cite{lugaresi2019mediapipe}. Because the hand and the parts are in one
image, the fingertip is a pixel of the very frame that is segmented: no second camera, no
calibration between cameras, no mirroring.

\paragraph{The model.} MediaPipe Hands is a two-stage pipeline. A palm detector runs on the full
frame and returns an oriented box around the hand; a landmark model runs on that crop and returns
21 hand landmarks, a handedness and a presence score. On later frames the crop follows the
previous landmarks, and the detector runs again only when the presence score drops. The Gesture
Recognizer adds a gesture-embedding model and a classifier on the landmarks, whose canned head
returns one of seven gestures or none. The models are Google's, used as distributed; we did not
retrain them. We run the recogniser in video mode on the camera's $1280\times720$ colour stream
at 30~Hz, for one hand, with a detection confidence of 0.6, and take landmark 8, the index
fingertip, as the pointing pixel.

\subsection{Finger-Pointing Camera Aim}
\label{sec:mc_aim}
Before a frame is captured, the camera must look at the part. Jogging the robot from a pendant
breaks the hands-free cycle, so the operator points at the part instead, and the camera turns
until the fingertip is in the middle of the image. Given the fingertip pixel of an image that is
already about 0.1~s old when the gesture is recognised, the goal is a smooth, safe motion that
centres it. The key design choice is to restrict the motion to a rotation about the camera's own
optical centre: the camera's position is held, so the operator steers only where it looks. This
restriction is what makes the measurement easy.

\paragraph{Error with exact latency compensation.} The fingertip pixel $(u,v)$ of the image taken
at $t_k$ is turned into a ray in the base frame with the camera orientation at $t_k$, from a
history of joint states, filtered there by a One-Euro filter \cite{casiez2012oneeuro}, and
expressed in the camera frame of the current control cycle:
\begin{equation}
\vect{r}_B = \Rf{B}{C}(t_k)\,\frac{\mathbf{K}^{-1}[u\ v\ 1]^{\top}}{\norm{\mathbf{K}^{-1}[u\ v\ 1]^{\top}}},
\qquad
\vect{d} = \Rf{B}{C}(t)^{\top}\,\vect{r}_B,
\qquad
\vect{e} = \big(d_x/d_z,\ d_y/d_z\big).
\label{eq:aim_error}
\end{equation}
\textit{Proposition.} If the camera only rotates about its optical centre $\vect{c}$ and the
fingertip $\vect{x}$ is still, then $\vect{r}_B=(\vect{x}-\vect{c})/\norm{\vect{x}-\vect{c}}$ does
not depend on the camera's orientation, so Equation~\ref{eq:aim_error} gives the exact current
error from an old image, whatever the fingertip's depth. \textit{Proof.} The measured ray, rotated
by $\Rf{B}{C}(t_k)$, is the base-frame direction from $\vect{c}$ to $\vect{x}$; neither point
moves. If the optical centre drifts by $\Delta\vect{c}$, the direction changes by at most
$\norm{\Delta\vect{c}}/\norm{\vect{x}-\vect{c}}$ radians. $\square$ The latency is therefore
compensated, not predicted, and the image delay leaves the loop. A ray more than about $73^\circ$
off the optical axis ($d_z<0.3$) is ignored.

\paragraph{Control law.} An image-based visual servo \cite{chaumette2006vs} in its simplest form,
a proportional loop on the angular error with a deadband:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\omega}_C = \Big(-K\,s\,e_y,\ K\,s\,e_x,\ -k_r\,\psi\Big),
\qquad
s=\frac{\norm{\vect{e}}-0.2\,\epsilon}{\norm{\vect{e}}},
\label{eq:aim_law}
\end{equation}
with $K=2.5~\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, $k_r=1.5~\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, both rates capped at $15^\circ$/s.
Two rotations aim the optical axis; the third, the roll
$\psi=\operatorname{twist}_z\big(\Rf{B}{C_0}^{\top}\Rf{B}{C}\big)$ about the optical axis relative to
the home view $C_0$ (taken from a swing--twist split), is redundant and is held at home so that the
image stays upright for the gesture recogniser. The loop engages when $\norm{\vect{e}}$ exceeds
$\epsilon$, 5\,\% of the image width, and releases inside $0.4\,\epsilon$. Measuring the rate from
$0.2\,\epsilon$, inside the release radius, is what lets it stop: measured from the release radius
itself, the camera crept towards it for ever and never handed the arm back. There is no integral
term, because it winds up over the latency, and no derivative term, because a hand landmark is
noisy. The command is smoothed by two first-order lags of 60~ms with a $60^\circ/\mathrm{s}^2$
cap, so the acceleration is continuous, and a component that would take the optical axis beyond
$\pm35^\circ$ of pan or $\pm25^\circ$ of tilt from home within the next 0.35~s (about the
stopping time from full rate) is dropped, while one that brings it back is kept.

\paragraph{Gain and stability.} For one axis, the loop is an integrator with gain $K$ in series
with a lag $\tau$, from the smoother and the arm: $L(s)=K\,e^{-s\tau}/s$. Its gain crosses over at
$\omega_c=K$, and its phase margin is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PM}=\frac{\pi}{2}-K\tau .
\label{eq:aim_pm}
\end{equation}
With $\tau\approx0.15$~s and the latency compensated, $K=2.5~\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ leaves a margin of
about $68^\circ$; if the compensation failed and $\tau$ grew to 0.25~s, it would still leave about
$54^\circ$. The gain was raised from $1.2~\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, sized for the uncompensated loop, once
the latency was compensated.

\paragraph{From the camera rate to joint rates.} The flange moves so that the optical centre
stays still, with a small proportional pull ($k_p=2~\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, capped at 20~mm/s) back to
where it started; damped least squares \cite{wampler1986dls} with $\lambda=0.02$ gives the joint
rates, scaled uniformly so that no joint exceeds 0.5~rad/s:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\vect{v}_E &= k_p\,(\vect{c}_{0}-\vect{c}) + \boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\vect{p}_E-\vect{c}),
\qquad \boldsymbol{\omega} = \Rf{B}{C}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_C,\\
\dot{\vect{q}} &= \mathbf{J}^{\top}\big(\mathbf{J}\mathbf{J}^{\top}+\lambda^2\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}
\begin{bmatrix}\vect{v}_E\\ \boldsymbol{\omega}\end{bmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:aim_dls}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Execution and safety.} A human in the loop needs a streaming interface, not a
trajectory: the joint rates go to the driver's velocity controller, which the aim switches in when
it starts and out, back to the trajectory controller, once the arm is still. We chose our own
Jacobian solve on the velocity controller over MoveIt Servo, because the collision model and the
branch lock (\S\ref{sec:mc_reach}) run in every cycle anyway and Servo would add its own
singularity handling and smoothing on top. Every cycle, the pose 0.3~s ahead is checked against
the collision model (table, fixtures, arm and tool; no parts are registered yet), the branch lock
and the wrist singularity. A fingertip lost for 0.3~s ramps the rate to zero, any other gesture
stops the aim, and a force above 15~N or joint states older than 0.2~s abort it. The aim starts
only from a still arm with the camera within 5~cm of its home position, and never while the
multi-view refinement runs.

\paragraph{Command interpolation.} The control law runs at 125~Hz and the robot's controller at
500~Hz. The first version streamed the rates at 50~Hz, and near the image centre the wrist buzzed
audibly. Our first hypothesis was that a proportional-only loop was not enough. A recording of the
joint states at 500~Hz against the commands showed instead a staircase: each new velocity was a
step held for ten controller cycles, which the arm applied as a torque kick, and adding an
integral or a derivative term would not have changed it. Between images, the error is recomputed
from the joint states, so the camera's 30~Hz is no limit on the command rate. The control rate was
raised to 125~Hz, and a relay written in C++ now sits between the node and the controller. When a
command $\dot{\vect{q}}_k$ arrives at $t_k$, it ramps linearly from the value it is sending,
$\dot{\vect{q}}_{\mathrm{out}}(t_k)$, over the measured command period $T$,
\begin{equation}
\dot{\vect{q}}_{\mathrm{want}}(t) = \dot{\vect{q}}_{\mathrm{out}}(t_k) + \operatorname{clip}\!\Big(\frac{t-t_k}{T},0,1\Big)\big(\dot{\vect{q}}_k-\dot{\vect{q}}_{\mathrm{out}}(t_k)\big),
\label{eq:relay}
\end{equation}
and publishes at 500~Hz, each step limited to 4~rad/s$^2$ and 0.6~rad/s. $T$ is a running average
of the gaps between commands (2--50~ms). The cost is one command period of delay (8~ms). If the
commands stop for 0.1~s, the relay ramps the arm to zero at 2~rad/s$^2$ by itself, which also
covers a hard kill of the aim node mid-turn, which previously left its last rate on the
controller.

\label{alg:aim}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

\ENDIF
\end{algorithmic}

\section{Perception and Registration}
\label{sec:mc_perception}
Once the camera looks at a part, the next challenge is to say which part it is and where it is,
to the millimetre. Given one RGB-D frame and the CAD library, the goal is the part's model $m$
and its pose $\Tf{C}{O}$. Three families of methods address this problem (\S\ref{sec:bg_pose}).
Classical model-based pipelines match local 3D features of the CAD model and the scene and refine
the result by ICP \cite{drost2010ppf,birdal2015ppf,rusu2009fpfh}; they need no training, but they
struggle with clutter and with the thin, featureless faces of plates. Instance-level learned
estimators are accurate, but they must be trained on every object, and our library changes
whenever a part is printed. Recent novel-object estimators take a CAD model at test time and need
no training on it \cite{wen2024foundationpose,lin2024sam6d}. In our application there is a CAD
model for every part, the parts are untextured, and the library grows; therefore, we use a
novel-object, CAD-based estimator. We split the problem into three stages, each solved by the tool
that suits it: SAM2 segments the part the operator points at, point-pair features decide which CAD
model it is, and FoundationPose estimates its pose. An earlier version of the cell ran SAM-6D
\cite{lin2024sam6d} for all three at once; it needed the part's identity in advance and took over
a minute per request, against seconds for the present pipeline. Figure~\ref{fig:mc_perception}
shows the stages and where each runs.
\figplaceholder{fig:mc_perception}{From one frozen RGB-D frame to a saved pose: clicks $\to$ SAM2
mask $\to$ masked cloud (N $\le$ 2000) $\to$ PPF scores per CAD $\to$ FoundationPose pose

\subsection{Capture and Clicks}
\label{sec:per_capture}
The capture gesture freezes one RGB-D pair (colour and depth aligned, their stamps at most 0.25~s
apart) and shows the frozen colour image to the operator, who picks points on it. Each point is a
pixel with a label, \emph{part} or \emph{not this part}, and the set is tied to the frozen frame by
its time stamp; a set whose stamp does not match the held frame is refused, because the
alternative is segmenting a pixel nobody looked at. At least one positive point is required:
negatives only carve a mask, they cannot start one. One request carries the frame (colour as
8-bit PNG, depth as 16-bit PNG in millimetres, the live intrinsics) and the points to the
registration server.

\subsection{Segmentation with SAM2}
\label{sec:per_segment}
The segmentation stage must turn a few clicked pixels into the exact image region of one part,
for parts the model has never seen and that may touch other parts. Classical alternatives (depth
discontinuities, plane removal and clustering, region growing) fail where parts touch flush or lie
on the table, which is exactly the welding case; a promptable segmentation model needs no
knowledge of the part and is steered by the clicks. We use SAM2 \cite{ravi2024sam2}, in its
version 2.1 with the small Hiera encoder, through its image predictor.

\paragraph{The model.} SAM2 extends the Segment Anything model \cite{kirillov2023sam} from images
to videos (Figure~\ref{fig:sam2_arch}). An image encoder, Hiera \cite{ryali2023hiera}, a
hierarchical vision transformer pretrained as a masked autoencoder, runs once per image; a feature
pyramid fuses its coarser stages into the image embedding, while its finer stages are passed
directly to the decoder for detail. A prompt encoder, identical to SAM's, represents each click by
a positional encoding of its pixel plus a learned embedding of its type, so a positive and a
negative click differ only in that embedding. A mask decoder of two-way transformer blocks lets the
prompt tokens and the image embedding attend to each other, upsamples, and outputs masks with a
predicted quality (IoU) each; for an ambiguous prompt such as a single click it returns several
candidate masks. For video, a memory encoder and a memory bank carry the masks of earlier frames;
on a single image the memory is empty and the model behaves like SAM. The model was trained on the
SA-1B images and the SA-V video set (50.9k videos, 35.5M masks); we did not fine-tune it.
\figplaceholder{fig:sam2_arch}{SAM2 architecture, image path only (image encoder, prompt encoder,
mask decoder), adapted from \cite{ravi2024sam2}}{The SAM2 architecture.}

\paragraph{How we use it.} The input is the frozen colour image and the clicked points with their
labels; depth is not used. With a single click, SAM2 is asked for its candidate masks and the one
with the highest predicted quality is kept, because one click is ambiguous between the whole part,
one face and a hole; with several clicks it returns one mask. The output is a binary mask at the
image's resolution. A mask that spills onto a neighbour or the table is carved back with a
negative click, which is why the interface offers \emph{not this part}. The authors name thin
structures and nearby look-alike objects as failure modes; in the cell these are a plate seen
edge-on and two plates of similar size, both handled by extra clicks.

\subsection{Part Classification by Point-Pair Features}
\label{sec:per_classify}
The registration stage needs the right CAD model, and the operator should not have to name it.
Given the segmented cloud, the goal is the library model that explains it best. We cast this as
model-based verification: every candidate model proposes poses that would explain the cloud, and
the model whose best pose explains it best wins. Point-pair features \cite{drost2010ppf} suit this
because they need no training and use exactly what a CAD model provides, surface points with
normals. For two oriented points $(\vect{m}_1,\vect{n}_1)$, $(\vect{m}_2,\vect{n}_2)$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}(\vect{m}_1,\vect{m}_2) = \big(\norm{\vect{d}},\ \angle(\vect{n}_1,\vect{d}),\
\angle(\vect{n}_2,\vect{d}),\ \angle(\vect{n}_1,\vect{n}_2)\big),\qquad
\vect{d}=\vect{m}_2-\vect{m}_1,
\label{eq:ppf}
\end{equation}
is invariant to rigid motion. Offline, the features of all point pairs of a model are quantised and
stored in a hash table. Online, a scene reference point paired with every other scene point looks
up its features; each matching model pair fixes the pose up to one rotation $\alpha$ about the
normal and casts a vote for (model point, $\alpha$), and the accumulator's peak gives a full pose
(a generalised Hough transform) \cite{drost2010ppf}. We use OpenCV's implementation of this method.

\paragraph{Model and scene clouds.} Each CAD model is sampled with 60\,000 area-uniform surface
points and exact face normals, then thinned to about 600 points by keeping one representative per
voxel; averaging would round the box edges and create normals that belong to no face. It is trained
with a sampling step of 0.04 of its diameter (finer for thin curved parts), distance bins of 0.04
of its diameter and 30 angle bins ($12^\circ$). The scene cloud is the masked depth, back-projected
with $\mathbf{K}$: the mask is eroded by 2 pixels, because edge pixels mix the part's depth and the
table's; normals are estimated from 12 neighbours on a slightly dilated crop and turned towards the
camera, since the model's normals point outwards and the angle features are sign-sensitive; the
cloud is thinned to a voxel of $\max(D/60,\,1.5~\mathrm{mm})$ and at most 2000 points. Fewer than
20 points means no answer.

\paragraph{Size filter.} A model is voted only if its diameter fits the cloud's,
\begin{equation}
1-0.55\ \le\ D_{\mathrm{scene}}/D_m\ \le\ 1+0.15,
\label{eq:ppf_extent}
\end{equation}
because a partial view can be much smaller than its part, but not larger. On the first real frame
tested, a looser upper bound let a 206~mm model compete against a 255~mm segment and win on score;
at 0.15 it is rejected outright. If every model is rejected, the filter is ignored. The filter also
turns a CAD exported in the wrong unit into an ``unclassifiable'' answer instead of a wrong one.

\paragraph{Verification.} Vote counts are not comparable across models: a peak's height grows with
the model's point count and its self-similarity. Therefore the votes rank hypotheses only within a
model, and identity is decided by a normalised two-sided score. For each model, the 12 most-voted
poses are refined by ICP (50 iterations; identical for every candidate, so it cannot bias the
ranking), and each is scored by
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
s = \mathrm{cov}\cdot\mathrm{expl},\\
\mathrm{cov}=\frac{|\{\vect{y}\in\mathcal{Y}:\ d(\vect{y},\mathcal{V})\le\tau\}|}{|\mathcal{Y}|},\qquad
\mathrm{expl}=\frac{|\{\vect{x}\in\mathcal{V}:\ d(\vect{x},\mathcal{Y})\le\tau\}|}{|\mathcal{V}|},
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:ppf_score}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{Y}$ is the scene cloud, $\mathcal{V}$ the model points visible from the camera
at that pose (a back-face test and a coarse z-buffer), $d(\cdot,\cdot)$ the distance to the nearest
point of a set and $\tau=8$~mm, about the depth noise. The coverage is the fraction of the scene the
model explains; the second factor is the fraction of what should be visible that is actually there. Coverage alone lets a large model win by
blanketing the segment; the visible-model term alone lets a small one win by hiding inside it.
A model's score is the best over its hypotheses, the winner is the highest score, and the margin to
the runner-up is reported; a small margin is not an error but a signal that the distinguishing
feature is out of view. This is structurally Birdal and Ilic's verification \cite{birdal2015ppf},
with their occlusion discount replaced by an explicit visibility test; their coarse-to-fine
segmentation and pose clustering are not needed, because the SAM2 mask already isolates the part.
The PPF pose is discarded: it is scaffolding for the score, and FoundationPose estimates the pose
from scratch on the chosen model.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Part classification by point-pair features}
\label{alg:ppf}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE scene cloud $\mathcal{Y}$ with camera-facing normals; library models with trained hash tables
\ENSURE model name, score table, margin
\STATE $\mathcal{L}\gets$ models passing the size filter (Equation~\ref{eq:ppf_extent}); \textbf{if} $\mathcal{L}=\emptyset$ \textbf{then} $\mathcal{L}\gets$ all models
\FORALL{models $m\in\mathcal{L}$}
  \STATE vote with every fifth scene point as reference; keep the 12 most-voted poses
  \FORALL{kept poses $\mathbf{T}$}
    \STATE refine $\mathbf{T}$ by ICP; $\mathcal{V}\gets$ model points visible at $\mathbf{T}$; score by Equation~\ref{eq:ppf_score}
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE $s_m\gets$ the best score of model $m$
\ENDFOR
\STATE \textbf{return} $\arg\max_m s_m$, all $s_m$, and the margin to the runner-up
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{6D Pose Registration with FoundationPose}
\label{sec:per_register}
With the part segmented and identified, the registration stage must estimate its pose from one
RGB-D frame and its CAD model, without training on that part. We use FoundationPose
\cite{wen2024foundationpose} in its model-based setting, in which a CAD model is given at test time.

\paragraph{The model.} FoundationPose estimates a pose by render-and-compare, in three steps
(Figure~\ref{fig:fp_arch}). First, it generates pose hypotheses: the translation is initialised at
the median depth inside the detected region, and the rotations are sampled from viewpoints on an
icosphere around the object, each combined with several in-plane rotations. Second, a refiner
improves each hypothesis: the CAD model is rendered at the hypothesis, the observed RGB-D is cropped
around the hypothesis's projection (so the crop itself carries the translation error), and a shared
convolutional backbone followed by two transformer encoders predicts a translation and a rotation
update in the camera frame, applied in a disentangled form,
\begin{equation}
\vect{t}^{+}=\vect{t}+\Delta\vect{t},\qquad \mathbf{R}^{+}=\Delta\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{R},
\label{eq:fp_update}
\end{equation}
so that the translation step does not depend on the rotation step. Third, a hierarchical ranker
scores all refined hypotheses: each rendering and its crop give an alignment embedding, and
self-attention across the embeddings of all hypotheses scores each one relative to the others; the
best is returned. The networks were trained once, on about 1.2 million synthetic images of
Objaverse and Google Scanned Objects with textures generated from language-model descriptions,
rendered in Isaac Sim; they are object-independent, and we did not fine-tune them.
\figplaceholder{fig:fp_arch}{FoundationPose, model-based setting: hypothesis generation, the
render-and-compare refiner, the hierarchical ranker, adapted from \cite{wen2024foundationpose}}{The
FoundationPose pipeline.}

\paragraph{How we use it.} The inputs are the intrinsics, the colour image, the depth in metres,
the SAM2 mask (in place of the paper's 2D detector) and the chosen CAD mesh, scaled from
millimetres to metres; the output is $\Tf{C}{O}$, the part's CAD frame in the camera frame, and an
unnormalised ranking score. One estimator stays resident with its networks loaded, and switching
parts only swaps its mesh, which takes milliseconds; this is what lets PPF choose a different model
on every request. Registration runs five refinement iterations on about 252 hypotheses (42
viewpoints $\times$ 6 in-plane rotations in the released code; the paper uses 12 in-plane rotations,
504 hypotheses). Our CAD models carry no texture, so the renderings are flat grey and the comparison
rests on geometry and depth. The score is a ranking value of the order of tens, not a probability,
and is not used as a gate. Symmetry information is not given to the estimator; the cell handles
symmetric tubes itself (\S\ref{sec:per_symmetry}). The registered pose is put into the base frame by
Equation~\ref{eq:frame_chain} with the joint state at the frozen frame's time stamp, not at the
reply's, so the seed is exact even when the capture, the clicks and the registration took a minute.

\subsection{Live Tracking}
\label{sec:per_track}
After registration, the operator moves the part into its place. A registration costs seconds; the
part moves continuously. Therefore, the part is tracked between registration and saving with
FoundationPose's tracking mode, which runs only the refiner, from the last pose, on each new frame:
no hypotheses and no ranking. It runs on the laptop's GPU, in the same Docker image, next to the
camera, with two refinement iterations per frame.

\paragraph{Cancelling the camera's motion.} The camera may move while a part is tracked, and the
tracker must see only the part's own motion. Each frame carries $\Tf{B}{C}$ at its image stamp, and
before each refinement the previous pose is predicted into the new camera frame, assuming the part
stayed put in the base frame:
\begin{equation}
\Tf{C_{t}}{O} = \big(\Tf{B}{C_{t}}\big)^{-1}\,\Tf{B}{C_{t-1}}\,\Tf{C_{t-1}}{O}.
\label{eq:ego_motion}
\end{equation}
Each returned pose is put into the base frame at its own image stamp before it is used; poses in the
frame of a moving camera cannot be averaged.

\paragraph{Losing and recovering the part.} The tracker returns no confidence, so we compute one by
rendering the CAD model's depth $\hat{D}$ at the new pose and comparing it with the measured depth
$D$ over the rendered pixels $\mathcal{R}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{fit}=\frac{|\{u\in\mathcal{R}:\ |D(u)-\hat{D}(u)|\le10~\mathrm{mm}\}|}{|\mathcal{R}|}.
\label{eq:track_fit}
\end{equation}
A part is declared lost when the fit stays below 0.5 for three frames, and found again only at 0.6
or more; the hysteresis keeps a hand passing over the part from toggling the state. While lost, no
pose is published, the tracker retries cheaply from the last good pose on every frame, and every
3~s the laptop sends one frame to the desktop for a re-registration, with the CAD silhouette at the
last good pose as the mask and the tracked part's name forced, so that no click is needed. The name
is forced because a re-registration once reclassified the projected mask and answered with the plate
under the part; an answer that names another part is now refused. The fit has one blind spot: a flat
plate on a flat table fits the depth almost anywhere in its plane, so a wrong pose can score high.
\figplaceholder{fig:tracking_states}{Tracking states IDLE, TRACKING and LOST with their transitions
(start; fit $<$ 0.5 for 3 frames; fit $\ge$ 0.6; re-seed every 3 s; stop at save) and the
desktop/laptop data flow}{Tracking states and the registration--tracking split.}

\paragraph{Why not ICP tracking.} The first tracker ran point-to-plane ICP on every frame, on the
CPU, inside a crop box that followed the part. Even after its nearest-neighbour search was optimised
it ran at about 10~Hz, too slow to follow a part being moved by hand, and on a still part its pose
wandered by millimetres (Chapter~\ref{chp:results}). ICP is right for a stationary part, and it is kept for
the stationary steps below. Fusing the two trackers, for example in a Kalman filter, was considered
and dropped, because it would combine a fast tracker with a slow one that adds nothing while the part
moves. A tracker answers \emph{where is it now}; a registration answers \emph{where exactly is it}.
Each is used for its own job, and the pose is refined before it is frozen.

fp\_stream.predict\_ego, LatestSlot; notes/realtime\_fp.md S2--S9; thesis\_notes.md 2026-10-02,
10-06, 10-09.}

\subsection{Stationary Refinement and Saving}
\label{sec:per_stationary}
When the operator releases the part and gives the save gesture, its pose is frozen into the
assembly. The goal is the best estimate of a resting pose, from a tracker whose single frames
jitter. Taking the last tracked pose would be a single draw from that jitter. Instead, we take the
newest run of tracked poses that describe one resting place, the \emph{stationary tail}: walking back
from the newest pose (at most 75 frames, about 3~s), a pose joins while it stays within 8~mm and
$3^\circ$ of the tail, and three disagreeing poses in a row end it, which allows the part to be moved
by hand before saving. Its robust mean is
\begin{equation}
\bar{\vect{t}}=\operatorname{median}_i\,\vect{t}_i,\qquad
\bar{\mathbf{R}}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^{\top},\ \ \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top}=\operatorname{svd}\Big(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{R}_i\Big),
\label{eq:pose_mean}
\end{equation}
the chordal mean of the rotations, over the inliers $\mathcal{I}$: poses within 3.5 robust standard
deviations (from the median absolute deviation) of the median position on every axis and within
$3^\circ$ of the chordal mean. If fewer than $\max(3,\lfloor n/3\rfloor)$ poses agree, all are used.
The spread is recorded with the pose.

\paragraph{Save-time ICP.} The tracker's estimate is good enough to follow a part, not to weld it:
when saved poses came straight from the tracker, neighbouring parts interpenetrated by 6--11~mm.
Therefore, the averaged pose is refined by point-to-plane ICP \cite{chen1992p2plane} against five
fresh clouds of the part, from which the table and every saved part have been removed:
\begin{equation}
E(\mathbf{R},\vect{t}) = \sum_i w_i\,r_i^2,\qquad
r_i=\big(\mathbf{R}\vect{p}_i+\vect{t}-\vect{q}_i\big)^{\!\top}\vect{n}_i,
\qquad w_i = \exp\!\Big(-\frac{r_i^2}{2\nu^2}\Big),
\label{eq:p2plane}
\end{equation}
with $\vect{p}_i$ model samples, $\vect{q}_i$ their nearest scene points and $\vect{n}_i$ the scene
normals; a pair that fails the normal gate below gets zero weight. The Welsch weights follow Zhang et al.'s fast and robust ICP \cite{zhang2022fricp}: the
kernel width $\nu$ starts at three times the median residual and halves, stage by stage, down to a
noise floor estimated from the scene's own local flatness, and Anderson acceleration with a
monotone safeguard speeds up each stage. The indicator is a normal gate: a model point is paired
only with a scene point whose normal agrees within $60^\circ$,
\begin{equation}
(\mathbf{R}\,\vect{n}^{\mathrm{model}}_i)^{\top}\vect{n}^{\mathrm{scene}}_j \ge \cos 60^\circ .
\label{eq:normal_gate}
\end{equation}
The gate exists because of thin plates. The model is sampled on every face, but the camera sees only
some, and without the gate the hidden back face of an 8~mm plate paired with its visible front: the
model straddled its own two faces, leaned by $6.5^\circ$ and put the weld root 10~mm off, while the
fit looked fine. With the gate the same data gave a lean below $1^\circ$. A side effect is a useful
diagnostic: a plate seen from one side now has a fitness of about 0.5, and the old fitness of 1.0
was the warning sign. The five results are averaged robustly, and the ICP pose replaces the tracker's
only if its mean fitness is at least 0.2 and its correction at most 20~mm and $8^\circ$.

\paragraph{The static environment.} A saved part becomes part of the static environment: its CAD
samples at the saved pose, held in the base frame. Live points within a few millimetres of it are
removed from every later scene, so the next part's ICP cannot snap onto an already saved neighbour
when the two are pushed flush; the same geometry is the neighbour model of the refinement and the
obstacle model of the planner. The assembly file records every saved part with its model, pose,
pose statistics and the camera pose it was registered from.

\subsection{Symmetric and Cut Tubes}
\label{sec:per_symmetry}
A plain tube looks the same at any rotation about its own axis, so its spin is not observable; the
tracker and ICP each return an arbitrary one, and once ICP ``corrected'' a pipe by $30.8^\circ$, all
of it spin. Symmetry must be told to the estimator. The correction from the reference pose to the new
one is split into a swing, which tilts the axis $\vect{a}$, and a twist about it, and only the swing
is kept:
\begin{equation}
\Rf{}{\mathrm{ref}}^{\top}\Rf{}{\mathrm{new}} = \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{swing}}\,\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{twist}}(\vect{a}),
\qquad \Rf{}{\mathrm{saved}} = \Rf{}{\mathrm{ref}}\,\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{swing}},
\label{eq:swing_twist}
\end{equation}
with the twist taken from the quaternion of the correction, $\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{twist}}\propto(w,\,(\vect{v}^{\top}\vect{a})\vect{a})$.
The save gate then judges the swing angle only. This applies to uncut tubes; a cut end fixes the
spin, and a rounded-rectangle tube is only four-fold symmetric.

A tube cut at an angle (a mitre) or to fit another tube (a saddle) is not symmetric, but its spin is
still poorly observed: the cut is where it touches the part it stands on, out of the camera's sight,
and the rest of the tube looks the same at any spin. On one bench placement a mitred tube came back
with its axis right but its spin $36^\circ$ off, the cut $15^\circ$ off the plate, and the seam was
refused for a fit-up gap ranging over 26~mm. Neither single- nor multi-view measurement can fix
that spin; physics can. The tube rests on its base, so the right spin is the one that makes the cut
sit flat on it. With $\vect{x}_j$ the seam curve on the base, which does not depend on the spin, and
$(\phi_j,z_j)$ its angle and height in the tube's frame,
\begin{equation}
\psi^{*} = \arg\min_{\psi}\ \Big(\max_{j} g_j(\psi) - \min_{j} g_j(\psi)\Big),
\qquad g_j(\psi)=h_{\mathrm{cut}}(\phi_j-\psi,\,r_o)-z_j,
\label{eq:spin_settle}
\end{equation}
where $h_{\mathrm{cut}}(\phi,r)$ is the height of the cut at angle $\phi$ and radius $r$, so
$g_j$ is the axial gap between the cut and the base along the contact. The range, not the mean, is
minimised: a uniform gap is a height error that the other steps measure, while a varying gap is a
wrong spin. The settled spin is accepted only if it brings the range below 1.5~mm (or a quarter of
its value before) and improves it by at least 1~mm; the settled pose is saved, so that reachability
and marking use it too. The seam itself never depended on the wrong spin (a plane--cylinder
intersection is spin-free); the fit-up check that guarded it did.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Settling the spin of a cut tube on what it stands on}
\label{alg:spin_settle}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE a tube with a cut base (mitre or saddle); the parts it may stand on; pose tolerance $\tau_{\mathrm{pose}}$
\FORALL{candidate base parts $B$}
  \STATE $\vect{x}_j\gets$ the seam curve of the tube's outer wall on $B$; $(\phi_j,z_j)$ in the tube's frame
  \STATE grid search $\psi\in[-180^\circ,180^\circ)$ in $1^\circ$ steps, then $\pm1^\circ$ in $0.05^\circ$ steps, minimising Equation~\ref{eq:spin_settle}
  \STATE require the end to be on $B$: $\max_j|g_j|\le2\tau_{\mathrm{pose}}$
\ENDFOR
\STATE take the best base; accept if range$_{\mathrm{after}}\le\max(1.5~\mathrm{mm},\ \mathrm{range}_{\mathrm{before}}/4)$ and the gain is $\ge1$~mm
\STATE \textbf{if} accepted \textbf{then} $\Tf{B}{O}\gets\Tf{B}{O}\,\mathbf{R}_z(\psi^{*})$; save it to the assembly and the static environment
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Multi-View Close-Range Refinement}
\label{sec:mc_multiview}
A single view from the scan pose leaves millimetres of error where it matters most, at the joints,
which are seen from about half a metre and often at grazing angles. Two properties of the sensor
make close views worth their cost. The depth error grows with the square of the range
(Equation~\ref{eq:depth_error}). And the camera-side errors turn with the view: an error in the
extrinsic translation turns with the wrist, and a depth bias points along each line of sight, so
views from different directions disagree in a measurable way. The geometry of the parts adds a
third reason: a part is registered only as well as the flat faces it shows, and the side of a round
pipe seen from one direction leaves its position along the view loose. With all parts saved, the
robot therefore moves the camera to four close views around the joints and refines all poses
together. The run is one service call: plan the views, move to each, capture, return home, refine,
and rewrite the assembly. Everything after the capture is computation on data saved to disk, so a
run can be replayed offline with the same code, for example with another extrinsic.
\SRC{notes/multiview\_refine\_plan.md (design D1--D8); multiview.py, multiview\_capture.py,
multiview\_refine.py, multiview\_service.py, selfcal.py, part\_shapes.py; README \S15.}

\subsection{View Planning}
\label{sec:mv_views}
The views should see as much of the seam regions as possible, each region from more than one
direction, from poses the arm can reach on its locked branch without coming near anything. The
targets are the CAD surface samples of the saved parts: a sample 1--30~mm from another part lies in
the seam band, where the weld roots will be, and weighs 1; any other sample weighs 0.1; a sample
closer than 1~mm to another part is a touching face, hidden, and is dropped. The candidates look at
the centre of the assembly's bounding box from 0.40~m, at elevations of $45^\circ$ and $60^\circ$,
every $30^\circ$ of azimuth and every $90^\circ$ of roll: 96 poses. A candidate sees a target if the
target is between 0.28 and 0.60~m away, inside the $69^\circ\times42^\circ$ field of view, within
$60^\circ$ of incidence, and not behind another part's collision box. The views are picked greedily
with diminishing returns: with $c_i$ the number of picked views that already see target $i$, the
gain of a candidate $v$ is
\begin{equation}
g(v)=\sum_i w_i\,\rho_{c_i}\,\mathbf{1}[\,v\text{ sees }i\,],\qquad
(\rho_0,\rho_1,\rho_2,\rho_3,\dots)=(1,\,0.5,\,0.25,\,0,\dots),
\label{eq:view_gain}
\end{equation}
so a second and a third sighting of a target still count, and a fourth does not. Feasibility is
checked lazily, only for the candidates the greedy pick wants, because inverse kinematics and
collision checks cost far more than visibility. Among the rolls of a direction within 98\,\% of the
best gain, the one with the least joint travel from home is taken. The views are visited in
nearest-neighbour order in joint space, each joint vector taken as the $2\pi$-equivalent inside the
joint limits and the cable window (\S\ref{sec:mc_reach}).

Two elevations were left out on purpose. A $30^\circ$ view would halve the weakest uncertainty of
the bench T-joint, but none is reachable at 0.40~m on the bench and its candidates doubled the
planning time; a $75^\circ$ view sees the faces of a standing plate beyond $60^\circ$ of incidence and
adds nothing.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Planning the close-range views}
\label{alg:mv_views}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE saved parts (CAD samples, collision boxes), the current joints, the home pose
\STATE weight the targets: 1 in the seam band (1--30~mm from another part), 0.1 elsewhere; drop touching samples
\STATE 96 candidates: look-at poses at 0.40~m, elevations $\{45^\circ,60^\circ\}$, azimuths every $30^\circ$, rolls every $90^\circ$
\STATE keep the candidates that see at least one seam-band target
\FOR{$k=1$ \TO 4}
  \STATE rank the candidates by Equation~\ref{eq:view_gain}, excluding directions within $30^\circ$ of a pick
  \STATE take the best \emph{feasible} one: IK on the locked branch, wrist and elbow away from their singularities, arm and tool $\ge20$~mm from the parts, fixtures and table
  \STATE among its rolls within 98\,\% of the gain, prefer the least joint travel; update the sighting counts
\ENDFOR
\STATE order the views by nearest neighbour in joint space; plan the transits between them and home
\STATE \textbf{if} fewer than two views or no way home \textbf{then} refuse
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Capture and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:mv_capture}
Tracking is paused for the whole run, because a live crop would chase the moving camera. The arm
drives to each view with the same force-watched executor as the marking motion
(\S\ref{sec:mc_marking}), at a capped joint speed, waits 0.5~s after it stops, and takes the
per-pixel median of 16 fresh organized clouds, with the camera pose from the transforms at the
cloud's own stamp. The median cuts the temporal noise of a still scene before any geometry is done,
and frames are cheap. Before the arm moves, the extrinsic in use is checked against the calibration
file (to 0.1~mm), and a failed or aborted move stops where it is, with no automatic recovery motion.
Each view is stored in the camera frame. For the refinement it is cropped to 30~mm around the saved
parts, cut 2~mm above the table plane, thinned to a 3~mm voxel, given normals that face that view's
camera (the normal gate relies on it) and transformed into the base frame.

\subsection{Joint Refinement of Touching Parts}
\label{sec:mv_joint}
Refining the parts against the views is a registration problem, but not an ordinary one, because
the parts touch. The danger is not a gross jump, since the refinement starts from saved poses that
are already within a few millimetres; it is a quiet slide of a few millimetres towards a neighbour,
or along a direction the views do not measure. Four guards prevent it:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Ownership.} Each scene point belongs to the part with the nearest registered surface.
  A point farther than 15~mm from every part belongs to none, and a point within 4~mm of a second
  part is dropped: the strip where parts meet pulls both ways, and the root is defined by the faces
  on either side of it. Ownership is fixed within a solve and recomputed between rounds.
\item \textbf{A prior.} Each correction is penalised by its size, so the result is a
  maximum-a-posteriori estimate with the saved pose as the prior: where the views constrain the pose
  strongly the data win, and where they do not the part stays put.
\item \textbf{One-sided overlap.} A part may not sink into its neighbour, measured against the
  neighbour's true shape, but it may move away from it: a gap is information, not an error.
\item \textbf{Equal views.} Each view carries the same total weight, so the nearest or largest view
  does not dominate.
\end{enumerate}

For part $k$ with a correction $\boldsymbol{\xi}_k\in\mathfrak{se}(3)$, taken about the part's own
centre (a $1^\circ$ turn about the base origin, half a metre away, would move it 9~mm), the solve is
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\min_{\boldsymbol{\xi}_k}\ \sum_{v}\omega_v\!\sum_{i\in\mathcal{O}_k^v} w_i\,r_i^2
+ \sigma^2\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_k^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_k
+ w_o\sum_{c}\max\!\big(0,\,-\mathrm{sd}_c-\varepsilon\big)^2,\\
r_i=\vect{n}_i^{\top}\big(\mathbf{T}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_k)\vect{p}_i-\vect{q}_i\big),
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:mv_objective}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{O}_k^v$ are the points of view $v$ that part $k$ owns, paired with its CAD samples
$\vect{p}_i$ within 10~mm and through the normal gate; $\omega_v=1/N_v$ makes the views equal;
$w_i$ are Welsch weights whose width starts at the largest of three times the median residual, its
90th percentile and $\sigma$, and shrinks by 0.7 per iteration down to the point noise
$\sigma=1.5$~mm; $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\operatorname{diag}\big((1^\circ)^2\mathbf{I},(3~\mathrm{mm})^2\mathbf{I}\big)$
is the prior; and $\mathrm{sd}_c$ is the signed distance of contact sample $c$ (up to 200 of the
part's samples within 8~mm of a neighbour) to that neighbour, penalised with $w_o=10$ beyond
$\varepsilon=0.5$~mm of penetration. The 90th percentile is in the starting width because, when
most points slide along their faces with zero residual, the median says nothing, and the few faces
that do see the error (a plate's end faces) would be weighted away before the first step.
Equation~\ref{eq:mv_objective} is solved by Gauss--Newton, at most 30 iterations, and the parts take
turns (block coordinate descent): two rounds, the part with the most owned points first, stopping
early when no part moves more than 0.2~mm and $0.05^\circ$.

\paragraph{True shapes.} The neighbour in the overlap term is the neighbour's true shape, a signed
distance computed from its registered primitive: a slab is its box; a tube is its wall between the
outer and inner radii, with the bore hollow, a flat top and its cut base; a band is its wall along
the spine. The distance is exact on the faces and approximate near edges. Bounding boxes, used
before, are exact for plates and wrong for everything else: a branch tube that wraps round its main
tube sits deep inside the main tube's box, and the plate under a mitred tube lies inside the tube's.
\paragraph{No resting contact.} An option pulls each part's foot onto the face it stands on. It is
off: on synthetic data it closed a real 3~mm gap to under 1~mm without a warning. The views alone
determine a standing plate, and the gap is measured and reported instead
(\S\ref{sec:mv_accept}).

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Joint refinement of touching parts}
\label{alg:mv_refine}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE parts $k$ (CAD samples with normals, saved poses $\mathbf{T}_k^0$, true shapes); views (points, normals, camera rotations $\mathbf{R}_v$)
\STATE $\omega_v\gets1/N_v$; assign every scene point to its owner (dead band 4~mm, none beyond 15~mm)
\FOR{round $=1,2$}
  \FORALL{parts $k$, most owned points first}
    \STATE contacts $\gets$ up to 200 samples of $k$ within 8~mm of a neighbour
    \REPEAT
      \STATE pair owned points with $k$'s samples (within 10~mm, normal gate); Welsch weights; overlap rows for $\mathrm{sd}_c<-\varepsilon$; prior rows
      \STATE solve the linearised Equation~\ref{eq:mv_objective} about $k$'s centre; $\mathbf{T}_k\gets\exp(\boldsymbol{\xi})\,\mathbf{T}_k$
    \UNTIL{converged or 30 iterations}
    \STATE information $\mathbf{H}_k$ (Equation~\ref{eq:observability}); fitness; penetration; gaps of resting faces
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE recompute ownership; \textbf{if} no part moved more than 0.2~mm / $0.05^\circ$ \textbf{then break}
\ENDFOR
\STATE judge every part (Table~\ref{tab:mv_acceptance}); apply the fallback cascade
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Observability}
\label{sec:mv_observe}
A refinement must say not only where a part is but also which of its directions the views actually
measured. With $\vect{J}_i=\big[(\vect{p}_i\times\vect{n}_i)^{\top}/\rho,\ \vect{n}_i^{\top}\big]$ the
Jacobian of residual $i$, the rotation scaled by the part's size $\rho$ (the 90th-percentile radius
of its samples) so that all six directions are in millimetres, the data information of part $k$ and
the uncertainty of each of its eigen-directions are
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{H}_k=\sum_i\omega_i\,\vect{J}_i^{\top}\vect{J}_i,\qquad
\sigma_{\mathrm{dir}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\lambda}},
\label{eq:observability}
\end{equation}
and a direction counts as measured when $\sigma_{\mathrm{dir}}\le0.5$~mm. The criterion is absolute
on purpose. A first, relative version counted a direction as measured if it carried 5\,\% of the
strongest direction's information; because a plate's broad faces carry thousands of points, its
edges then looked unmeasured, and the in-plane rotation of a standing plate was refused although its
resting contact pinned it. Unmeasured directions are not frozen: the prior holds them, and they feed
the acceptance rule for relative poses below. A tube's spin about its own axis is named symmetric and
is exempt.

One limitation is known. The base plate's slide within its own plane varies by 1--3~mm from run to
run, although it counts as measured: it rests on grazing points along the plate's edges, which are
noisier and more correlated than the noise model assumes. It does not move the weld roots, but the
reported uncertainty is optimistic in that direction.

\subsection{Online Estimation of the Camera's Extrinsic Translation}
\label{sec:mv_selfcal}
An error $\vect{d}$ in the extrinsic translation, expressed in the camera frame, shifts the cloud of
view $v$ by $\mathbf{R}_v\vect{d}$, with $\mathbf{R}_v$ the camera's rotation in that view, and the
shift is different in every view. The joint refinement alone does not remove it: the part of the
shift that all views share stays in the poses, and the remainder pulls the parts in conflicting
directions. On rendered data with an injected $\vect{d}=(3,-2,1)$~mm, the uncorrected refinement
tilted the base plate by a wrong $1.2^\circ$, and the acceptance rules let it pass. Therefore,
$\vect{d}$ is estimated jointly with the corrections of all parts, from the points:
\begin{equation}
r_{v,i} = \vect{n}_i^{\top}\big(\mathbf{T}_k\vect{p}_i - \vect{q}_i + \mathbf{R}_v\vect{d}\big),
\qquad
\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{N}_{dd} - \mathbf{N}_{dx}\mathbf{N}_{xx}^{+}\mathbf{N}_{xd},
\label{eq:extrinsic_d}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{N}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}$ is the normal matrix of the stacked residuals in the
unknowns $(\boldsymbol{\xi}_1,\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_K,\vect{d})$ and the Schur complement $\mathbf{S}$
is the information about $\vect{d}$ that is left after the poses have been accounted for. Only the
eigen-directions of $\mathbf{S}$ with an uncertainty of 0.5~mm or better are kept; the others are
named and set to zero. A first version estimated $\vect{d}$ from the per-view poses of each part
instead; even from the true poses it returned $(7,13,-15)$~mm for an injected $(3,-2,1)$~mm. The
points, not the per-view poses, carry the information.

\paragraph{Correcting a run.} When $\norm{\vect{d}}$ exceeds 0.5~mm, the run takes the shift out of
its own data and refines again, up to four times, until the remaining estimate is below 0.5~mm:
\begin{equation}
\vect{q}\leftarrow\vect{q}-\mathbf{R}_v\vect{D}\ \ \text{(every point of view $v$)},\qquad
\vect{t}_k^{0}\leftarrow\vect{t}_k^{0}-\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{scan},k}\vect{D}\ \ \text{(every saved pose)},
\label{eq:selfcal_apply}
\end{equation}
with $\vect{D}$ the accumulated estimate and $\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{scan},k}$ the camera rotation of the
view part $k$ was registered from. Both corrections are needed. The saved poses carry the error of the
scan view, and correcting only the close views, while the prior still held the unmeasured
directions at the uncorrected saved poses, created a fake change of the fit-up of about 8~mm on the
bench. The correction limits are then judged against the corrected prior.

\paragraph{Correcting the calibration.} Each run records its estimate and its $3\times3$ information
$\mathbf{I}_r$ under a hash of the extrinsic file it was made with. Runs with the same file are
combined by their information,
\begin{equation}
\vect{d}^{*}=\Big(\sum_r\mathbf{I}_r\Big)^{+}\sum_r\mathbf{I}_r\vect{d}_r,
\qquad \vect{t}_{EC}\leftarrow\vect{t}_{EC}-\Rf{E}{C}\,\vect{d}^{*},
\label{eq:selfcal_combine}
\end{equation}
so a direction one run cannot see is filled by another, and a corrected file is written only when at
least three runs agree (spread and leave-one-out shift both within 1~mm). It is promoted by hand.
Repeated captures of one scene are not independent evidence: they show repeatability, not
correctness. And any run made before a depth recalibration learned the depth error. The weakest
direction is vertical: a shift that moves every view's cloud up or down together is, for views at one
elevation, almost indistinguishable from all parts sitting higher. On the runs of one day the online
correction lowered the parts by about 7~mm while the camera was known to place surfaces about 2~mm
high, an over-correction of 4--5~mm in height; those runs were therefore not promoted.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Extrinsic-translation estimate and online self-calibration}
\label{alg:mv_selfcal}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE refined parts and the views of one run; threshold 0.5~mm
\STATE solve Equation~\ref{eq:extrinsic_d} for $(\boldsymbol{\xi}_1,\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_K,\vect{d})$ by Welsch-weighted least squares
\STATE $\mathbf{S}\gets$ Schur complement; keep $\vect{d}$ along eigen-directions with $1/\sqrt{\lambda}\le0.5$~mm
\IF{$\norm{\vect{d}}>0.5$~mm}
  \STATE $\vect{D}\gets\vect{d}$
  \FOR{at most 4 iterations}
    \STATE correct views and saved poses by Equation~\ref{eq:selfcal_apply}; refine again (Algorithm~\ref{alg:mv_refine})
    \STATE estimate the residual $\vect{d}'$; \textbf{if} $\norm{\vect{d}'}<0.5$~mm \textbf{then break}; $\vect{D}\gets\vect{D}+\vect{d}'$
  \ENDFOR
\ENDIF
\STATE record $(\vect{D},\mathbf{I})$ with the extrinsic file's hash \COMMENT{the calibration file changes only by Equation~\ref{eq:selfcal_combine}, by hand}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Acceptance and Fit-Up}
\label{sec:mv_accept}
A refinement is applied when it stays within the limits; the principle is that a correction within
the limits is applied, and one beyond them is reported and not applied. Table~\ref{tab:mv_acceptance}
lists the rules.
\begin{table}[htbp]
\caption{When a multi-view correction is refused.}
\label{tab:mv_acceptance}
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{0.24\textwidth}L{0.68\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
Check & Refused when \\
\midrule
Correction size & beyond the camera's share, more than 10~mm or $5^\circ$; for an uncut tube, the tilt of its axis, not its spin (a spin over $0.5^\circ$ is reported) \\
Fit & fitness below 0.2 \\
Overlap & penetration into a neighbour over 1.0~mm \emph{and} more than 0.2~mm worse than in the saved assembly (over 1.0~mm but no worse: applied with a warning) \\
Relative pose & the pose between two touching parts changed by more than 2~mm or $1^\circ$, with more than 2~mm of a part's correction along directions its views did not measure: that relative pose is the fit-up \\
Camera & $\norm{\vect{d}}>0.5$~mm while online self-calibration is off: every part kept as saved \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}
A refused part keeps its saved pose, and every accepted part is refined once more against it; an
accepted part whose pose relative to a held one would still change beyond 2~mm or $1^\circ$ is held
too, until nothing changes. The assembly is refined together or not at all. The rules were shaped by
real failures in both directions. Before the depth was calibrated, the refinement failed safely: its
fit was three times worse than on synthetic data and the rules refused both parts, so the assembly was
not corrupted. Later, a mitred tube's correction was refused for overlap although its saved pose
overlapped its plate as much, and for a rotation that was mostly its symmetric spin; the overlap rule
now compares with the saved assembly, and a tube is judged by its axis.

\paragraph{Fit-up.} After the refinement, the gap of every face that rests on a neighbour (a
down-facing face opposite a neighbour's face) is measured along the whole contact, because an operator
who places the parts too far apart must be told. It is compared with the root-gap limit of
ISO~5817:2023, Table~1, no.~617 \cite{iso5817}, for fillet welds, with the throat $a=0.7\,t$:
\begin{equation}
h_{\max}=
\begin{cases}
0.1\,a+c_0', & t\le3~\mathrm{mm},\\
\min\big(c_1\,a+c_0,\ h_{\mathrm{cap}}\big), & t>3~\mathrm{mm},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:iso_gap}
\end{equation}
where, at levels B, C and D, $c_0'$ is 0.2, 0.3 and 0.5~mm, $c_1$ is 0.1, 0.2 and 0.3, $c_0$ is
0.5, 0.5 and 1~mm, and $h_{\mathrm{cap}}$ is 2, 3 and 4~mm.
We use quality level C, which gives 1.62~mm for 8~mm plates (1.06 at B, 2.68 at D). A gap over the limit
is a warning, never a rejection. The rule is the same function that WeldSet uses to grade its joints
(\S\ref{sec:ws_parts}).

\section{Seams from the Registered Assembly}
\label{sec:mc_seams}
With every part registered and refined, the next step is to find the seams. Seams are not detected
in the point cloud; they are computed from the registered CAD models. The reason is simple: once a
part's CAD model is registered, the static environment holds exactly that CAD model at its pose, so
any detector run on it could only rediscover, with error, what the poses already say. In WeldSet's
benchmark the corresponding regime, transferring the seam from a registered model, is the near-perfect
rung, because the model is the truth (Chapter~\ref{chp:results}). What a detector on the raw scan
could add, the real fit-up and any warping, is measured separately (\S\ref{sec:mc_fitup}). Each part
is therefore represented as one of WeldSet's exact primitives (Chapter~\ref{chp:method_weldset}), and
the seams follow from where the posed primitives meet, by WeldSet's geometry in a form adapted to
registered rather than constructed parts.

\subsection{CAD Parts as Primitives}
\label{sec:mc_registry}
A registry maps every CAD model to a primitive. The mapping is derived automatically from the mesh,
and verified, never guessed:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Slab.} The oriented bounding box, with its axes sorted so that $w$ is the thickness. It
  is exact when every vertex is a box corner (within 0.05~mm) and the volumes agree within 1\,\%; it is
  an \emph{envelope} when every vertex lies on the box surface and the mesh fills at least 90\,\% of
  it, as for a plate with small notches or tabs, whose deficit is recorded.
\item \textbf{Tube.} The sweep axis is the direction least spread by the area-weighted face normals
  (the smallest eigenvector of $\sum_fA_f\vect{n}_f\vect{n}_f^{\top}$); the lateral faces give the centre
  by a circle fit and must lie on two radii; each end is classified as flat, a plane (a mitre) or a
  cylinder (a saddle), each fitted within 0.05~mm. A flat top is required; the other end is the base,
  where the cut is.
\item \textbf{Swept slab.} A straight extrusion with two flat caps. A closed outline whose walls are
  equal is a rounded-rectangle tube, with WeldSet's exact rounded-rectangle spine. An open band of
  constant wall gets a clamped cubic B-spline spine, with 4 to 16 control points; control points and
  every point's curve parameter are solved together, started from linear fits alternated with
  re-projection, and the first spine within 0.025~mm wins.
\end{itemize}
Anything else is recorded as unsupported, with the measured reason; a band whose wall varies, for
instance, is refused with the range of its wall rather than approximated. Each entry is then verified
in both directions against its CAD model, the CAD vertices onto the primitive and the primitive's
vertices onto the CAD, with the volumes compared, within the 0.25~mm chord budget of WeldSet. Both
directions are needed: the first alone accepts an entry that is too long or too thick. The check
caught a drawing error: a printed band whose second side had been drawn as a sideways copy of the first
instead of an offset along the normal. Finally, at run time, an entry is \emph{stale} if any vertex of
its CAD model lies more than 0.5~mm from the primitive. A stale entry is refused loudly, because once a
replaced plate kept its old entry, turned by $90^\circ$, and no seam was found at all, without a word.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{A CAD model as an exact primitive}
\label{alg:registry}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE a CAD mesh (mm)
\FORALL{derivations in the order slab, tube, swept slab}
  \STATE fit the primitive's parameters and its pose in the CAD frame
  \STATE \textbf{if} it fits within 0.05~mm (and its own volume or fill rule) \textbf{then return} the entry
\ENDFOR
\STATE \textbf{return} unsupported, with every derivation's reason
\STATE offline: verify CAD$\to$primitive and primitive$\to$CAD within 0.25~mm, volume within 1\,\%
\STATE at run time: refuse the entry if any CAD vertex is more than 0.5~mm off the primitive (stale)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Planar Seams}
\label{sec:mc_planar}
Two planar faces with unit normals $\vect{n}_1,\vect{n}_2$ through points $\vect{p}_1,\vect{p}_2$
meet on the line
\begin{equation}
\vect{x}(s) = \vect{x}_0 + s\,\hat{\vect{d}},\qquad
\hat{\vect{d}} = \frac{\vect{n}_1\times\vect{n}_2}{\norm{\vect{n}_1\times\vect{n}_2}},\qquad
\vect{n}_i^{\top}\vect{x}_0 = \vect{n}_i^{\top}\vect{p}_i\ \ (i=1,2),
\label{eq:plane_intersection}
\end{equation}
clipped to where both faces exist. WeldSet's accessibility rule (\S\ref{sec:ws_d4}) judges such a pair
under a contact tolerance, because its parts touch by construction. Registered parts do not touch: on
the bench, the standing plate of a T-joint came back 8--11~mm inside the base in one run and 1--8~mm
above it, tilted, in the next, because a single view cannot observe a thin plate sliding in its own
plane. The seam line, however, is the intersection of the two face \emph{planes}, and it is invariant
to exactly that slide. Therefore, the cell judges each face pair with a pose tolerance,
$\tau_{\mathrm{pose}}=10$~mm, the relative pose error of two registrations, in place of the contact
tolerance, and never rejects a seam for its fit-up:
\begin{itemize}
\item the line is clipped to both faces with the tolerance as slack only on the in-face axis that the
  line keeps nearly constant, so a tilted member keeps its seam as far as it stays within the tolerance
  and the seam is never extended along its own length (the strict clip kept 44 of 250~mm of a tilted
  plate's seam);
\item each face must reach past the other's plane by more than the tolerance; a face that lies behind
  it is the wrap-around phantom of a plate's own underside and is dropped, and one that falls short is
  kept as non-weldable (a lapping sheet thinner than the tolerance cannot be told from a penetrating
  one);
\item the fit-up is the signed distance of each face's near edge to the other's plane, positive for a
  gap and negative for penetration, reported with the seam;
\item a seam is non-weldable, with its reason, if the dihedral is out of range, if it is shorter than
  10~mm, or if no torch axis about the bisector clears the scene at five points along it
  (Equation~\ref{eq:ws_axes}).
\end{itemize}
Rejected candidates are kept, with their reasons, because the planner must avoid them. WeldSet's
post-passes then remove a fillet that runs along a butt seam's centreline and cross-runs against the
joint direction, and every seam is oriented so that its dominant component is positive, so that the
two fillets of one joint count their tacks from the same end.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Planar seam of two registered faces}
\label{alg:planar_seam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE faces $(A,f_a)$, $(B,f_b)$ of two posed primitives; pose tolerance $\tau=10$~mm
\IF{the faces are parallel}
  \STATE coplanar: WeldSet's butt and edge candidate; facing within $\tau$: a non-weldable record; \textbf{return}
\ENDIF
\STATE line $\gets$ Equation~\ref{eq:plane_intersection}; clip to both faces with slack $\tau$ on the near-constant axis
\STATE \textbf{if} the clipped run is empty or the line is farther than $\tau$ from the faces \textbf{then return} no seam
\STATE extent of each face past the other's plane: \emph{behind} $\to$ no seam; \emph{short} $\to$ non-weldable
\STATE fit-up $\gets$ signed distances of the near edges to the other plane
\STATE reason $\gets$ dihedral out of range, or shorter than 10~mm, or no clear torch axis at 5 points, or none
\STATE \textbf{return} the seam with class, polyline, approach axis, fit-up, weldable $=$ (reason is none)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Curved Seams}
\label{sec:mc_curved}
Pairs that involve a tube or a band meet along curves, computed by intersecting the posed primitives'
surfaces with WeldSet's verification constructors (\S\ref{sec:ws_curves}). A tube standing on a plate
meets it along a plane--cylinder intersection, an ellipse, or a circle when the tube is square to the
plate. With the tube axis $\vect{a}$ through $\vect{c}$, outer radius $r$, orthonormal
$\vect{u},\vect{v}\perp\vect{a}$, and the plate's face $\vect{n}^{\top}\vect{x}=\vect{n}^{\top}\vect{p}_0$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\vect{x}(\theta) &= \vect{c} + r(\cos\theta\,\vect{u}+\sin\theta\,\vect{v}) + h(\theta)\,\vect{a},\\
h(\theta) &= \frac{\vect{n}^{\top}(\vect{p}_0-\vect{c}) - r\,(\vect{n}^{\top}\vect{u}\cos\theta+\vect{n}^{\top}\vect{v}\sin\theta)}{\vect{n}^{\top}\vect{a}} ,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ellipse_seam}
\end{equation}
valid when $\vect{n}^{\top}\vect{a}\neq0$; the cell requires the tube to stand at least $10^\circ$
off the face. A branch tube on a main tube meets it along a saddle (Equation~\ref{eq:ws_saddle}), and a
band on a plate meets it along its two walls: each wall is an offset of the spine, traced along the
band's extrusion axis $\vect{z}_w$ onto the plate, $\vect{x}=\vect{p}_0+\lambda\vect{z}_w$ with
$\lambda=-(\vect{n}^{\top}\vect{p}_0+d)/(\vect{n}^{\top}\vect{z}_w)$, which is exact at any tilt
because the wall is a ruled surface along that axis.

Which end of a tube stands on the plate is found, not assumed: both ends and both faces of the plate
are tried, and an end counts when its axial distance to the curve stays within the pose tolerance all
round. Every point of the curve carries its own frame: the face normal $\vect{n}_a$, the member's wall
normal $\vect{n}_b$ (radial for a tube), and the approach
$\hat{\vect{b}}=(\vect{n}_a+\vect{n}_b)/\norm{\vect{n}_a+\vect{n}_b}$, which rotates along a curved
seam; the dihedral varies along the curve, from $65^\circ$ to $115^\circ$ round a $25^\circ$ mitre.
The verdict is WeldSet's torch cone, tested at every point: the seam is weldable when at least 95\,\% of
its points clear. A tube's bore is an inner fillet and a negative here, unless it is at least 200~mm
wide, because the pen holder and the camera are wider than a welding torch, for which WeldSet uses
80~mm. A curve that runs off the plate is refused as such, and a pair for which no rule exists yet (a
band on a tube) is reported, not dropped.

Curved seams tolerate registration error for the same reason planar ones do: a plane--cylinder curve
does not change if the tube slides along its axis or sits a few millimetres above or inside the plate.
A pose error shows up as a range of fit-up along the curve, never as a different seam.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Curved seam of a tube on a plate, or of a branch on a tube}
\label{alg:curved_seam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE a posed tube and a posed plate (or main tube); pose tolerance $\tau$
\FORALL{plate faces $F$ and tube ends $E$}
  \STATE require the tube on $F$'s outer side, its axis $\ge10^\circ$ off the face
  \STATE curve $\gets F\cap$ outer cylinder (Equation~\ref{eq:ellipse_seam}; the saddle for a tube), 1 point/mm
  \STATE gap $g_j\gets$ axial distance from the curve to $E$ at every point
\ENDFOR
\STATE keep the $(F,E)$ with the smallest $\max_j|g_j|$, if it is $\le2\tau$; else no seam
\STATE per point: $\vect{n}_a$, $\vect{n}_b$, approach $\hat{\vect{b}}$, dihedral
\IF{$\max_j|g_j|>\tau$}
  \STATE reject, reported as a near miss with its gap range
\ELSIF{a point lies off the plate}
  \STATE reject as off the plate edge
\ELSE
  \STATE weldable if the torch cone clears at $\ge95\,\%$ of the points
\ENDIF
\STATE record the bore as an inner seam, a negative unless it is $\ge200$~mm wide
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Fit-Up and Its Reporting}
\label{sec:mc_fitup}
Every seam carries its fit-up: for a planar seam the signed distances of the near edges, for a curved
one the range of the gap along the curve. The seam rule rejects on the pose tolerance, never on the
fit-up, because a penetration is registration error, not geometry. What the rule does instead is report:
a curved pair whose gap reaches beyond the tolerance, but not beyond twice it, is kept as a near miss
with its numbers. The rule exists because a branch tube on its main tube once gave the right saddle,
$69.4^\circ$ against the $70^\circ$ drawn, but one end of its gap reached 10.5~mm against the 10~mm
tolerance, and the pair was dropped without a word. A fallback that only logs makes a failure look like
a result. An unexpected fit-up gap is also a check of the scene: an unmodelled 7~mm plate under a part
showed first as a 0.9--4.6~mm fit-up gap, before it showed as early pen contacts. The comparison with
the root-gap limit of ISO~5817 (Equation~\ref{eq:iso_gap}) is made after the multi-view refinement,
where the gaps are best measured.

\paragraph{Fallback.} The cell keeps the edge detector it began with, a radius-PCA classifier of crease
points in the static environment cloud, for a part without a registry entry. It is used only when
allowed, and it says so: once, a new module missing from the installed package made every curved part
fall back to it silently, and a test now guards the install list.

\section{Tack Placement and Ordering}
\label{sec:mc_tacks}
Tacks follow WeldSet's tack rule, tackrule-0.1 (\S\ref{sec:ws_tacks}, Equations~\ref{eq:tackrule_len}
and~\ref{eq:tackrule_n}), applied unchanged to the weldable seams, with $t$ the gauge
of the thinnest workpiece of the assembly: a 3~mm tube wall gives 12~mm tacks, an 8~mm plate 32~mm. On an
open seam both effective ends are tacked and the rest are spaced evenly between them. A closed seam has
no ends: it gets an even number of tacks, at least four, evenly spaced from a phase that the rule derives
from a hash of the scene and the seam, so the layout is deterministic but does not depend on how the
curve happens to be parametrised. In the cell the scene's identifier is the directory the assembly was
saved in, so a closed seam's tack phase changes when the same assembly is saved again.

Each tack carries two numbers. Its \emph{tack number} counts along its seam from the seam's start, and is
the label it is shown and reached by. Its \emph{order} is the weld sequence of the scene: the ends first,
then the middles by recursive bisection, with the seams of one class interleaved so that heat would
alternate between them. On a curved seam each tack also carries its own approach axis, interpolated from
the seam's per-point frames at its arc length. The pen has no heat to balance, so it visits the tacks
seam by seam in tack-number order, which is the shorter route; the weld order is kept in the file for the
day a torch replaces the pen.

\section{Motion Planning and Execution}
\label{sec:mc_motion}
The last stage brings the pen to every tack. Three choices shape it. First, the collision model: the
scene is a handful of exact primitives whose poses we already hold, and a sampling planner's validity
test runs hundreds of thousands of times per plan; therefore we use our own model on capsules, boxes
and a plane, rather than a general mesh-based planning scene, which remains the upgrade path once
fixtures with real shapes enter the cell \cite{liu2023rcim}. Second, the descent: the registration
error we first measured was 4--11~mm, so a position-controlled pen would have gone 5~mm into the plate
or stopped 5~mm above it; a force-gated descent turns that error into a number instead of a crash, and
the number is the validation. Third, the mark: a dot measures, a stroke along the seam demonstrates.
Every plan is made on one arm configuration and checked before anything moves.

\subsection{Collision Model}
\label{sec:mc_collision}
The arm is six capsules on its joint frames (the base, the shoulder, the upper arm and the forearm,
displaced by the URDF's shoulder and elbow offsets, and the first two wrist links), with radii of
50--75~mm that cover the links with a margin. The tool is the envelope of the pen holder and camera
mount designed and printed for this work (Chapter~\ref{chp:setup}), derived from its CAD model with a
3~mm radial margin: a box for the flange adapter, two capsules for the holder (radii 48 and 20.5~mm), a
capsule of 8~mm radius for the pen that ends at the tip, a box for the camera arm and a box for the
camera body, measured on the bench. The parts are their primitives as boxes:
\begin{itemize}
\item a slab is its box;
\item a tube is six full-diameter boxes rotated by $30^\circ$ about its axis, each of half-width
  $r\sin15^\circ$, whose union covers the disc and stands at most $r\big(\sqrt{1+\sin^2 15^\circ}-1\big)$,
  3.3\,\% of $r$, proud of the wall, where a single box stood 41\,\% proud at its corners, exactly where
  $45^\circ$ tacks sit;
\item a cut tube is 12--36 radial sectors, each starting at its own lowest point of the cut, because one
  box from the cut's lowest point would hang 39~mm below a $25^\circ$ mitre, under the plate;
\item a band is a chain of boxes along its spine, each grown while its width stays within the wall plus
  1~mm.
\end{itemize}
The table is the touched, tilted plane (\S\ref{sec:cal_table}), and fixtures are boxes standing on it,
entered as a few pen-tip points on their tops. Distances are computed in closed form between capsules;
between a capsule and a box by a golden-section search along the capsule's segment, exact to
$10^{-4}$ of its length because the distance to a convex set is convex along a line; and against the
table from each primitive's lowest point. A box against a box is an intersection test only, so a tool
box such as the camera body gets no clearance against a part box, only ``not touching''; this is a
known limitation. The pairs checked are the arm and the tool against the parts, the fixtures and the
table, and the tool against the base, shoulder and upper arm; full self-collision is not modelled,
because the branch lock keeps the arm in one configuration.

\paragraph{Clearances.} On the bisector of a fillet with opening $\theta$, a capsule of radius $r_h$
whose axis ends a distance $\ell$ up the pen from the tip clears both plates by
\begin{equation}
c_{\mathrm{holder}}=\ell\sin\frac{\theta}{2}-r_h .
\label{eq:holder_clearance}
\end{equation}
With the holder first modelled as a single capsule ($r_h=42$~mm, $\ell\approx61$~mm), a square T-joint
was cleared by about 1~mm, and some seams counted as reachable only because a wrong registration had
opened the corner; the envelope derived from the holder's CAD model ($r_h=20.5$~mm, $\ell\approx51$~mm)
clears it by 15.5~mm. The tack clearance of 3~mm dates from the single-capsule model, under which a
square fillet allowed at most about 3~mm; with the envelope from the CAD model it is a margin on top of
an envelope that already carries 3~mm of its own. Free motions keep 20~mm, because a transit planned with the tack clearance
once skimmed a standing plate and, with the parts about 10~mm off their models, hit it at 9~N; near a
tack the free-motion clearance is lowered to 90\,\% of the end pose's own clearance, never below the
tack clearance, so that a transit can still start beside a plate. At a tack, the pen may touch the part,
and the table under a thin plate, but never reach the table itself.

\paragraph{A C++ port.} The planner calls the validity test from several threads, and in Python one
plan spent most of its minutes in it. The model was therefore ported to C++ (exposed to Python through
pybind11, the interpreter lock released during planning) and built from a specification exported by the
Python model, which remains the single source of truth. The port was tested where the two are most
likely to differ: on random and near-contact configurations, bisected towards the collision boundary,
it agrees exactly on validity and to $10^{-9}$ on distances, and it is about 590 times faster. The test
found a bug in the binding's output array and one exact tie decided by the last bit. Motions are checked
at a resolution of 0.01~rad per joint, about 5~mm of tip travel, so an 8~mm plate cannot slip between
two checks.

\subsection{Reachability}
\label{sec:mc_reach}
Before anything moves, every tack is solved: can the pen tip get there along its axis, with the arm on
one configuration, and with the approach and the descent clear? Inverse kinematics is solved on one
branch, the elbow-up configuration of the scan home: the signs of the lift, elbow and second wrist
joints are locked, solutions on another branch are discarded, and the arm never flips between tacks.
Each solution is found by damped least squares from the previous solution, the home pose and six random
seeds around it.

The pen axis of a tack is its seam's approach axis, tilted by a work angle
$\vartheta\in\{0^\circ,\pm10^\circ,\pm20^\circ\}$ about the seam tangent; a tilt away from the closer
plate is what makes a tack on a leaning plate reachable. The pen is axisymmetric, so its roll $\varphi$
about its own axis is free. That one degree of freedom is spent on reachability and on keeping the arm
elbow-up, never on a branch flip. A pair $(\vartheta,\varphi)$ is admissible for a tack when the tool
clears the scene at the tack and at the approach point 35~mm out along the pen (checked first on the
tool alone, before any inverse kinematics), both poses have a solution on the branch less than 1.2~rad
apart, and the straight descent between them, checked at six steps, stays continuous and clear.

\paragraph{Straight seams.} The pen's frame is the same at every tack of a straight seam, so one
$(\vartheta,\varphi)$ is chosen for the whole seam, from five tilts and twelve rolls: the one with the
largest smallest clearance over all its tacks, then the least joint travel between consecutive approach
poses, with a penalty for poses near the wrist singularity.

\paragraph{Curved seams.} Round a curved seam the pen's frame rotates with the approach axis, and one
roll per seam fails: on a tube's closed seam the wrist would have turned $115^\circ$ between two tacks.
Each tack therefore gets its own $(\vartheta,\varphi)$. Choosing each for clearance alone, however, put
consecutive approach poses $65$--$90^\circ$ apart on three joints, and the transits between them swung
the pen up to 0.9~m off. The pairs of a seam are therefore chosen together, by dynamic programming (a
Viterbi recursion \cite{forney1973viterbi}) over the admissible pairs of each tack:
\begin{equation}
C_k(j)=\min_{i}\Big[C_{k-1}(i)+\norm{\mathbf{W}\,\delta\big(\vect{q}_{k-1,i},\vect{q}_{k,j}\big)}_2\Big]
+\kappa\,\max\!\big(0,\ \delta_0-c_{k,j}\big),
\label{eq:roll_dp}
\end{equation}
where $\vect{q}_{k,j}$ is the approach pose of tack $k$ with its $j$-th admissible pair, $c_{k,j}$ its
clearance, $\delta$ the joint difference taken the short way round (except for the last wrist joint,
which stays in its window), $\mathbf{W}=\operatorname{diag}(2,2,1.5,1,1,0.5)$ the joint weights of the
transit planner, $\delta_0=10$~mm and $\kappa=0.15$~rad/mm, so that 1~mm of clearance below 10~mm is
worth about $9^\circ$ of base-joint motion. The chain starts at home or at the previous seam's last
approach, and the best sequence is recovered by backtracking. The recursion is exact and costs
$O(nJ^2)$ for $n$ tacks with $J$ admissible pairs each. A tack with no admissible pair is reported with
the reason of its best attempt, and skipped; the unreachable tacks are of the expected kinds, a concave
fillet tighter than the holder, or the far side of a tube with no elbow-up solution.

\paragraph{The cable window.} The camera's cable wraps round the wrist. Taking each new pose as the
$2\pi$-equivalent nearest the previous one, for continuity, wound the last wrist joint to $-202^\circ$
and $-287^\circ$ over one curved seam, and the cable's pull stopped two descents with a false 1.5~N
``contact'' 84~mm above the part; every good contact of the day before had that joint within
$\pm151^\circ$ of home. Every pose is therefore taken as the $2\pi$-equivalent nearest the previous one
\emph{among those within $\pm180^\circ$ of home}. The window loses no tool pose, since one full turn of
the last joint fits in it, and it keeps the cable slack.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Reachability with a locked branch}
\label{alg:reach}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE tacks of one seam; tilts $\{0,\pm10^\circ,\pm20^\circ\}$; rolls every $30^\circ$; tack clearance $c=3$~mm
\STATE \textsc{Try}$(k,\vartheta,\varphi)$: pose-only checks; IK of the approach and tack poses on the branch, in the cable window; $\norm{\Delta\vect{q}}_\infty\le1.2$~rad; descent clear at 6 steps
\IF{the seam is straight}
  \STATE for each $(\vartheta,\varphi)$: all tacks must pass and consecutive approach poses differ by $\le1.2$~rad per joint
  \STATE choose the largest smallest clearance, then the least joint travel (+ a wrist-singularity penalty)
\ELSE
  \STATE candidates of tack $k$ $\gets$ every $(\vartheta,\varphi)$ that passes \textsc{Try}
  \STATE run Equation~\ref{eq:roll_dp} from home (or the previous seam) over the reachable tacks; backtrack
\ENDIF
\STATE report every unreachable tack with the reason of its best attempt
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Descent and Departure}
\label{sec:mc_descent}
The descent of each tack is planned as a chain of inverse-kinematics poses every 2~mm along the pen
axis, from the approach point to the tack and on past it by a 5~mm overshoot, in case the real surface
lies beyond the registered one. Before the tack, every pose must clear the scene by the tack clearance,
except for the pen's allowed contacts. Past the tack, the pen is expected to touch, so the bound is
relaxed by exactly the distance travelled: moving $s$ along the pen changes any distance by at most $s$,
so a pose $s$ past the tack must keep the tack's clearance minus $s$. No surface can lie beyond the
measured table, so an overshoot that reaches the table ends there instead of refusing the tack; on a
root tack of a thin plate, 5~mm of overshoot along a pen $35^\circ$ off vertical had reached it. If the
descent fails at the planned roll, the rolls $\pm30^\circ$, $\pm60^\circ$ and $\pm90^\circ$ away are
tried. From the approach point, a departure chain runs out along the pen to 100~mm in 5~mm steps, every
pose clear with the whole tool, and the transits start and end at these departure points, where the
tool is in open space. Where the departure chain is blocked, the transit ends at the approach point
instead.

\subsection{Transit Planning}
\label{sec:mc_transit}
A transit is tried, in order, as:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the straight joint-space edge;
\item between two tacks, the tool's straight Cartesian line, its orientation interpolated by
  spherical linear interpolation, and then the same line bowed away from the joint as a quadratic
  B\'ezier curve whose midpoint is displaced by $\beta$:
  \begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
  \vect{x}(s) = (1-s)^2\,\vect{x}_0 + 2s(1-s)\,(\vect{x}_m + 2\beta\,\hat{\vect{b}}) + s^2\,\vect{x}_1,\\
  \hat{\vect{b}}=-\frac{\vect{z}_0+\vect{z}_1}{\norm{\vect{z}_0+\vect{z}_1}},\qquad
  \beta\in\{0, 30, 60, 100\}\ \mathrm{mm},
  \end{gathered}
  \label{eq:cartesian_bow}
  \end{equation}
  with $\vect{x}_m$ the chord's midpoint and $\vect{z}_0,\vect{z}_1$ the pen axes at the two ends, each
  pose solved on the branch at most 5~mm and $3^\circ$ from the previous, a joint jump over 0.3~rad
  counting as a failure;
\item OMPL's AnytimePathShortening \cite{luna2013aps,sucan2012ompl}: four RRT-Connect planners
  \cite{kuffner2000rrtconnect} in parallel threads, their solutions hybridised (spliced where they
  cross) and shortcut, for 3~s and then until the first exact solution, at most 10~s, in scaled joint
  coordinates $y_j=w_jq_j$, so that path length is weighted joint travel,
  \begin{equation}
  c(\text{path}) = \sum_{k}\norm{\mathbf{W}(\vect{q}_{k+1}-\vect{q}_k)}_2,\qquad
  \mathbf{W}=\operatorname{diag}(2,\,2,\,1.5,\,1,\,1,\,0.5),
  \label{eq:transit_cost}
  \end{equation}
  with the base and shoulder weighted most because they sweep the most space;
\item a single RRT-Connect in C++, then in Python, each followed by collision-aware shortcutting.
\end{enumerate}
Every path is re-checked at the nominal clearance, smoothed by a centripetal Catmull--Rom spline through
its waypoints (kept only if every sample is clear) and timed by time-optimal trajectory generation
\cite{kunz2012totg} at 0.3~rad/s and 0.5~rad/s$^2$, with every timed sample collision-checked and a
trapezoidal profile as the fallback.

\paragraph{Why this order.} We first planned every transit with APS alone, which minimises weighted joint
travel. Round a tube, two transits of nearly equal joint cost, 3.5 and 3.9~rad, moved the pen tip along
257 and 772~mm, and repeated runs between the same two poses varied from 0.26 to 1.6~m of tip travel.
Joint-space shortest is not what a cell should drive round a pipe: the quantity that matters is how far
the tool sweeps. The tool's straight line between departure points is the move a welder would program,
it is the same on every run, and between neighbouring tacks it is short; a sampling planner only has to
step in when that line is blocked. APS remains the fallback because, unlike asymptotically optimal
planners, it returns a solution quickly and improves it within a fixed budget, and on high-dimensional
arms it found shorter paths than RRT$^\ast$ in the same time \cite{luna2013aps}. It runs in C++ because
its Python bindings do not expose it and a Python validity callback would serialise its threads. The
planning margin, 2~mm above the clearance, is capped at half of the room the end poses themselves have:
uncapped, it made the end poses invalid, OMPL never started, and every transit fell back to the slow
planner. Safety margins compose. At execution, a planned transit is joined from where the arm actually
stands instead of being re-planned.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{The transit cascade}
\label{alg:transit}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE start and goal joints (goal in the cable window); clearance $c_t=\max\big(c,\ \min(20~\mathrm{mm},\,0.9\,c(\vect{q}_a),\,0.9\,c(\vect{q}_b))\big)$
\STATE \textbf{if} the straight joint edge is clear (checked every 0.01~rad) \textbf{then return} it
\IF{the transit joins two tacks}
  \FORALL{$\beta\in\{0,30,60,100\}$~mm}
    \STATE IK chain along Equation~\ref{eq:cartesian_bow}; \textbf{if} every pose and edge is clear \textbf{then return} it
  \ENDFOR
\ENDIF
\STATE margin $\gets\operatorname{clip}\big(\min(2~\mathrm{mm},\,(\min_{\mathrm{ends}}c-c_t)/2),\,0\big)$
\STATE path $\gets$ APS at $c_t+$ margin (Equation~\ref{eq:transit_cost}); re-check at $c_t$
\STATE \textbf{if} no path \textbf{then} path $\gets$ RRT-Connect (C++, then Python) + shortcut
\STATE smooth (spline, kept if clear); time by TOTG (every sample checked; else a trapezoid)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Force-Gated Descent and Marking}
\label{sec:mc_marking}
At the departure point, with the arm at rest, the force bias is measured over 0.5~s; the sensor's zero
drifts, by 7~N after one power cycle, which alone would trigger the abort limit. The pen then moves along
its own axis: at 50~mm/s to the approach point 35~mm before the tack, at 20~mm/s, and at 2~mm/s in the
last 6~mm before it and through the overshoot. The speeds follow from the pen being rigid: once the
threshold is crossed, the force keeps growing for the stopping time, at a rate set by the speed and the
part's stiffness. The descent is one trajectory goal on the robot's trajectory controller, polled every
2~ms and cancelled at the first sample where
\begin{equation}
\norm{\vect{F}(t)-\vect{F}_{\mathrm{bias}}} > F_{\mathrm{touch}} = 1.5\ \mathrm{N},
\qquad
\delta_c = (\vect{p}_{\mathrm{reg}}-\vect{p}_{\mathrm{tip}})^{\top}\vect{a},
\label{eq:touch}
\end{equation}
and the joints at the cancel are the contact record. The threshold is three times the sensor's noise of
about 0.5~N. The contact depth $\delta_c$ says how far before ($+$) or past ($-$) the registered point the
surface was met: an independent measurement of the registration along the pen axis, made with the
robot's kinematics and without the camera. A descent that runs through its whole overshoot is recorded
as no contact. Any motion is aborted above 8~N, and a move away from a contact above 40~N, since a pen
stopped on a rigid part can press harder than 8~N and must still be able to leave. A motion starts only
if its first point is within 0.35~rad of the current joints.

The mark is a dot, held for 0.5~s if the force is moderate, or a stroke along the tack. A stroke runs in
10~mm chunks at the contact depth, and between chunks the depth is corrected from the force along the pen,
by $2\times10^{-5}$~m/N times the difference to the holding force, at most 0.2~mm per chunk; two chunks
under 0.4~N mean the pen has lifted off, and the stroke stops. On a curved seam each tack draws its own
piece of the curve, since one pen axis cannot draw a loop round a tube. A stroke follows the registered
seam, so on a tight curve it climbs the real wall and reports more error than the touch itself;
therefore accuracy is measured with dots, and strokes demonstrate. After the mark, the pen retracts along
its axis to the departure distance, and the contact, the force and the depth are written to the marking
record.

A force-gated descent senses everything that pulls on the tool, and the camera cable is part of the tool:
the false contacts of the cable wind-up (\S\ref{sec:mc_reach}) read exactly like a touch.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Force-gated descent and the contact record}
\label{alg:descent}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE the planned descent (departure $\to$ approach $\to$ tack $+$ overshoot); $F_{\mathrm{touch}}=1.5$~N
\STATE at rest at the departure point: $\vect{F}_{\mathrm{bias}}\gets$ mean force over 0.5~s
\STATE time the descent: 50~mm/s to the approach point, 20~mm/s, 2~mm/s in the last 6~mm
\STATE \textbf{require} the first point within 0.35~rad of the current joints; send the goal
\WHILE{the goal runs (poll every 2~ms)}
  \STATE $f\gets\norm{\vect{F}-\vect{F}_{\mathrm{bias}}}$
  \STATE \textbf{if} $f>8$~N \textbf{then} cancel and fail
  \STATE \textbf{if} $f>F_{\mathrm{touch}}$ \textbf{then} cancel; contact $\gets$ (joints now, $f$); \textbf{break}
\ENDWHILE
\STATE \textbf{if} contact \textbf{then} tip $\gets$ FK(joints); $\delta_c$ by Equation~\ref{eq:touch} \textbf{else} record no contact
\STATE mark: a dot, or a stroke in 10~mm chunks with force-corrected depth
\STATE retract along the pen to the departure distance (only $f>40$~N stops it); write the record
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Architecture}
\label{sec:impl_arch}
The cell runs on two machines (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}). The desktop, with the RTX 5070 Ti, runs
the registration server in a Docker container that holds SAM2, the PPF library and FoundationPose; it
answers one HTTP request per registration, so only one frame per part crosses the network, over a
private overlay network (\S\ref{sec:setup_software}). The laptop, with the RTX 4060 mobile GPU, runs ROS~2 Jazzy, the camera, the robot driver,
every node of the cell and a second container of the same image that only tracks. The tracker and its
ROS client talk over a WebSocket on the same laptop, because the container runs Ubuntu 22.04 and
Python 3.10 without ROS, while the workspace runs ROS~2 Jazzy on Python 3.12, and ROS~2 Humble, the
distribution of the container's system, does not interoperate with Jazzy over DDS. A frame is one binary message (a small header with the sequence number, the
image stamp, the intrinsics and the camera pose, then the raw colour and depth bytes); at most two frames
are in flight, and the server keeps only the newest waiting frame, so a slow GPU drops frames instead of
building a backlog.

\subsection{The Cell as a State Machine}
\label{sec:impl_states}
One assembly passes through the states of Figure~\ref{fig:cell_states}: idle; aiming; frozen (clicking);
registering; tracking; saved, then the next part or refining; seams and tacks computed; planned; marking
tack by tack; done. Every transition has a failure edge, and every failure leaves the cell in a state from
which the operator can continue: a refused registration keeps the frozen frame for another try; a lost
part is re-seeded or re-registered; a refused refinement keeps the saved poses; an unreachable tack is
reported and skipped; an aborted motion stops where it is, with no automatic recovery motion; and every
motion node starts in a dry-run mode until it is told otherwise.

\subsection{Performance-Critical Components}
\label{sec:impl_cpp}
Three components are written in C++: the collision model and the planners, as a Python extension, which
turned the validity checks of one plan from minutes into seconds; the time-optimal trajectory generation,
as a service around MoveIt's implementation that needs no robot model; and the 500~Hz rate relay of the
aim. Everything else is Python. The rule that governs both is the same: what the Python side computes is
geometry, and time comes from the C++ side.

\subsection{Failing Loudly}
\label{sec:impl_loud}
Several of the incidents above were silent failures: a calibration solve that failed without a residual,
a seam step that fell back to an older detector because a module was missing from the installed package,
a near-miss seam dropped without a word, a stale registry entry that produced no seam. The implementation
therefore follows one rule: a step that cannot do its job refuses with its reason, and a fallback says
that it ran. The install list of the package is tested, registry entries are checked against their CAD
models at run time, near misses are reported with their numbers, every refinement reports which parts it
kept and why, and the motion nodes default to a dry run.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:mc_summary}
To conclude, the proposed cell reduces perception to one task, measuring the poses of known parts, and
computes everything else from exact geometry. Calibration is referenced to the robot itself, link by link
and in order. Registration combines three pretrained or classical tools, each for the stage it suits,
and the cell adds what they lack: verification across models, a confidence for tracking and recovery
when it fails, robust saving, symmetry handling and settling by physics. The multi-view refinement
corrects the poses jointly, estimates the camera's extrinsic translation on the way and refuses what it
cannot measure. Seams and tacks are WeldSet's geometry at the measured poses, and the motion stage spends
the pen's free roll on reachability, keeps the tool's path short and turns the remaining registration
error into a measured contact depth. Chapter~\ref{chp:method_weldset} describes the other side of the same
geometry, the dataset in which these seams are the ground truth, and Chapter~\ref{chp:results} reports
how well each component performs.
